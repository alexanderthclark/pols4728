Using attention in research

What can we learn from the output?

An attention heatmap describes a particular computation: for one head, in one layer, each destination forms a weighted mixture of source value vectors. A large weight tells us that the source receives a large mixing coefficient. Its effect on a particular classification also depends on the value vector and everything that follows.

The Infinite Jest schematic uses two reviews to motivate sentiment classification. The DistilBERT chapter reports measured predictions and attention weights for those exact reviews. The residual path carries each token’s current vector forward, while attention and the position-wise MLP add updates. A sentiment readout uses the final token representations.

That makes attention useful for investigating the relationships a model has learned. For example, Clark et al. (2019) found BERT heads associated with syntactic relations and coreference. Such patterns can suggest what to inspect or test next. A pattern across token positions and an explanation of a particular sentiment score answer different questions.

A useful sentiment exercise

Begin with a fixed target, such as the positive-class logit, and show the original prediction. Start with the Infinite Jest reviews above, then compare “Infinite Jest was good” with “Infinite Jest was not good.” Rerun the classifier on each complete sentence. These further edits are proposed checks; the saved DistilBERT results include the original reviews and the interventions shown above.

Put the chosen attention row beside the score changes and ask whether its highlighted positions help explain them. Inspect negation as a phrase: in a deep transformer, the representation at “good” may already contain information from “not.” Removing a token also changes the context, positions, and potentially every attention row. It does not isolate a unique contribution inherent to that word.

Integrated gradients can provide a signed attribution to a chosen output relative to a specified baseline; SHAP requires a specified treatment of missing features. Both address questions that a raw attention heatmap leaves open, and both need their own interpretation. For an introductory exercise, a small set of meaningful text edits makes the contrast easier to inspect. See Sundararajan et al. (2017) and Lundberg and Lee (2017).

From words to marketing touchpoints

LiDDA predicts conversion from a sequence of marketing interactions and uses aggregated, normalized attention to assign relative touchpoint credit. Its single attention layer and flattened readout are deliberate design choices. The paper also reports sensitivity checks, comparison with incremental propensity, MMM calibration, and email experiments. These provide evidence beyond the attention visualization itself. LiDDA v3, §§2.1, 2.4, 2.7, 3.2 and Appendix B.2.

A 40% credit share is a model-based allocation within a path. It is not automatically a 40% change in conversion probability or a 40% causal effect. The Infinite Jest example illustrates the interpretive distinction; it is not a reconstruction of LiDDA.

A technical qualification about LiDDA

The paper describes aggregation across heads and normalization, but does not fully specify how its query-by-source attention tensor reduces to one credit per touchpoint. We therefore leave that query-axis reduction unspecified here.

What to remember about Q, K and V

A query is what a destination is matched on; a key is what each source is matched on; a value is what that source contributes to the mixture. In a trained transformer, these projections are learned from the training task. “Looking for” is an analogy, not a claim that their coordinates have human-readable meanings. The socks-and-shoes calculation makes this matching and mixing explicit; the trained-model experiments test how it affects predictions.

The exact socks-and-shoes model

The target is 0 for socks before shoes and −1 for shoes before socks. Each input row contains a socks indicator, a shoes indicator, and a position value: +1 in the first slot and −1 in the second. This is a constructed model with fixed parameters, not a pretrained language model.

We form by adding token embeddings and position vectors of the same width. Position embeddings can be learned without reserving a coordinate. For this example, we choose fixed vectors: the token embeddings are zero in the third coordinate, and the position vectors are zero in the first two. From a constructed head to a pretrained model

A useful next step is Neel Nanda’s pretrained one-layer attention-only model. Its configuration has eight parallel attention heads and no feed-forward network in the layer. Embeddings, positional information, normalization, and a prediction readout remain. One layer need not mean one head.

Sources and conventions

The opening distinguishes a token’s shared lookup embedding from its position-dependent input and later contextual representations. The DistilBERT implementation used for the measured chapter adds word and position embeddings, then normalizes them before the transformer blocks. The desert diagrams are conceptual; their routes and sense labels are not measured model outputs.

The primer then separates learned projection matrices from quantities computed for each input. Within a head, determine query–key matching, while transform the content that is mixed and written back. The attention matrix is computed from the current representations, not stored as a free parameter. Training differentiates through this computation to learn the projections and upstream parameters. Parameters remain fixed during inference; attention weights are recomputed. Attention and MLP updates refine the running token vectors across layers; they can also cancel or overwrite features. These diagrams omit normalization and biases.

Goro Kobayashi and colleagues (2020), Attention is Not Only a Weight, motivates examining transformed values as well as attention weights. Samira Abnar and Willem Zuidema (2020), Quantifying Attention Flow in Transformers, explains why repeated mixing complicates attribution to original input tokens.

Ashish Vaswani and colleagues (2017), Attention Is All You Need, §3.2, defines the matching and weighted-value operation. Sarthak Jain and Byron Wallace (2019), Attention is not Explanation, and Sarah Wiegreffe and Yuval Pinter (2019), Attention is not not Explanation, motivate testing an explanation against the architecture and prediction behavior. Their disagreement is useful: attention can be diagnostic without every heatmap being a faithful word-importance explanation.

Nelson Elhage and colleagues (2021), A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits, especially “Attention Heads as Information Movement,” the QK/OV decomposition, and the tensor-product appendix. The distinction between moving information across positions and transforming it within each position motivates this lesson.

Simon J. D. Prince (2023), Understanding Deep Learning, chapter 12, §§12.2–12.3, equations 12.6–12.9. Prince stores tokens as columns. We transpose the convention: projection matrices multiply from the right, attention multiplies from the left, and softmax normalizes each row. The paper’s per-token maps likewise transpose when translated into this convention.

Additional visual reference: Grant Sanderson, Attention in transformers, step-by-step. Its emphasis on what information a destination seeks and how values change a representation informs this sequence. That lesson uses column vectors; this one retains tokens as rows.
