Using attention in research

What can we learn from the output?

An attention heatmap describes a particular computation: for one head, in one layer, each destination forms a weighted mixture of source value vectors. A large weight tells us that the source receives a large mixing coefficient. Its effect on a particular classification also depends on the value vector and everything that follows.

The opening schematic uses two sentences to motivate the task. The following DistilBERT chapter reports measured predictions and attention weights for those exact sentences. The residual path preserves each token’s representation while attention and the position-wise MLP add updates. A sentiment readout uses the final token representations. The numerical example removes those extra paths to make one exact decomposition possible.

That makes attention useful for investigating the relationships a model has learned. For example, Clark et al. (2019) found BERT heads associated with syntactic relations and coreference. Such patterns can suggest what to inspect or test next. A pattern across token positions and an explanation of a particular sentiment score answer different questions.

A useful sentiment exercise

Begin with a fixed target, such as the positive-class logit, and show the original prediction. Compare “the film was awful” with “the film was great”; then compare “the film was good” with “the film was not good.” Rerun the classifier on each complete sentence. These further edits are proposed checks for a trained model. The DistilBERT chapter reports measured results for the Infinite Jest sentences; the three-token example uses hand-constructed parameters.

Put the chosen attention row beside the score changes and ask whether its highlighted positions help explain them. Inspect negation as a phrase: in a deep transformer, the representation at “good” may already contain information from “not.” Removing a token also changes the context, positions, and potentially every attention row. It does not isolate a unique contribution inherent to that word.

Integrated gradients can provide a signed attribution to a chosen output relative to a specified baseline; SHAP requires a specified treatment of missing features. Both address questions that a raw attention heatmap leaves open, and both need their own interpretation. For an introductory exercise, a small set of meaningful text edits makes the contrast easier to inspect. See Sundararajan et al. (2017) and Lundberg and Lee (2017).

From words to marketing touchpoints

LiDDA predicts conversion from a sequence of marketing interactions and uses aggregated, normalized attention to assign relative touchpoint credit. Its single attention layer and flattened readout are deliberate design choices. The paper also reports sensitivity checks, comparison with incremental propensity, MMM calibration, and email experiments. These provide evidence beyond the attention visualization itself. LiDDA v3, §§2.1, 2.4, 2.7, 3.2 and Appendix B.2.

A 40% credit share is a model-based allocation within a path. It is not automatically a 40% change in conversion probability or a 40% causal effect. Our sentiment model illustrates the interpretive distinction; it is not a reconstruction of LiDDA.

A technical qualification about LiDDA

The paper describes aggregation across heads and normalization, but does not fully specify how its query-by-source attention tensor reduces to one credit per touchpoint. We therefore do not present the sentiment model’s exact computation as LiDDA’s implementation.

What to remember about Q, K and V

A query is what a destination is matched on; a key is what each source is matched on; a value is what that source contributes to the mixture. In a trained transformer, these projections are learned from the training task. “Looking for” is an analogy, not a claim that their coordinates have human-readable meanings. Learn the flow from matching to mixing to prediction first. The full calculation follows below.

The exact socks-and-shoes model

The target is 0 for socks before shoes and −1 for shoes before socks. Each input row contains a socks indicator, a shoes indicator, and a position value: +1 in the first slot and −1 in the second. This is a constructed model with fixed parameters, not a pretrained language model.

We form by adding token embeddings and position vectors of the same width. Position embeddings can be learned without reserving a coordinate. For this example, we choose fixed vectors: the token embeddings are zero in the third coordinate, and the position vectors are zero in the first two. We follow this one construction through the calculation.

Queries and keys read token identity; values carry position. With , the score matrix is . Shoes assigns 75% to socks and 25% to itself. The socks row is uniform. All weights come from finite softmax scores.

Swapping the token order preserves the identity-based matching rule while changing which position is carried by each token. The mean output changes from +0.25 to −0.25, giving predictions 0 and −1. Removing positional information or using uniform attention gives −0.5 for both orders. This exact solution covers the two sequences shown; it does not demonstrate generalization to arbitrary clothing sequences.

From a constructed head to a pretrained model

A useful next step is Neel Nanda’s pretrained one-layer attention-only model. Its configuration has eight parallel attention heads and no feed-forward network in the layer. Embeddings, positional information, normalization, and a prediction readout remain. One layer need not mean one head.

Sources and conventions

Goro Kobayashi and colleagues (2020), Attention is Not Only a Weight, motivates examining transformed values as well as attention weights. Samira Abnar and Willem Zuidema (2020), Quantifying Attention Flow in Transformers, explains why repeated mixing complicates attribution to original input tokens.

Ashish Vaswani and colleagues (2017), Attention Is All You Need, §3.2, defines the matching and weighted-value operation. Sarthak Jain and Byron Wallace (2019), Attention is not Explanation, and Sarah Wiegreffe and Yuval Pinter (2019), Attention is not not Explanation, motivate testing an explanation against the architecture and prediction behavior. Their disagreement is useful: attention can be diagnostic without every heatmap being a faithful word-importance explanation.
