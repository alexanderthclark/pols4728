Fine-tuning for writing style

Teach an embedding model what “similar” means

Ecclesiastes and Orwell’s academic parody can convey related ideas while sounding very different. A sentence that reverses the verse can sound much more like it. Follow those texts through a schematic fine-tuning example that teaches an encoder to measure style.

Fine-tuning for writing style

Carry the argument with you

  • Semantically related passages can differ in style; stylistically similar passages can make opposite claims.
  • A shared encoder processes every member of a triplet, and its parameter updates can change every text embedding.
  • Triplet loss compares an anchor–positive distance with an anchor–negative distance and a margin.
  • A schematic geometric improvement is not evidence of model performance; evaluate held-out texts and styles.

1. Meaning is not the only similarity

“The race is not to the swift” and Orwell’s academic rendering of Ecclesiastes express related ideas in very different styles. “The swift shall win the race” reverses the claim while preserving much of the verse’s vocabulary and cadence. Which relationship should an embedding preserve?

2. Separate the claim from the style

A, from Ecclesiastes 9:11 (KJV): “the race is not to the swift”. B, an excerpt from Orwell’s academic parody: “…success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity…”. C, a constructed counterexample: “the swift shall win the race”. D, a new academic paraphrase for this lesson: “Competitive success accrues to the participant exhibiting the greatest velocity.”

A and B convey related ideas, rather than exactly equivalent meanings. C is not scripture, and D is not an Orwell quotation. Color identifies register; shape identifies the claim. Each text keeps its letter.

Orwell presents his rendering in Politics and the English Language and acknowledges that it loses detail. Only a short excerpt appears here. These four passages are not the StyleDistance training data.

3. Begin with semantic neighbors

Suppose a starting representation places A near B because their claims are semantically related, and C near D for the same reason. That geometry can serve meaning-based retrieval. For a style task, however, we want A nearer C, even though C reverses A’s claim.
This is a conceptual starting geometry. No claim is made that an actual pretrained encoder yields these coordinates or treats negation this way.

4. Use one encoder three times

Use A as the anchor, C as the style-positive, and B as the style-negative. Each passage passes through the same encoder with shared parameters θ. The resulting three vectors enter one training objective.
za = fθ(A),   zp = fθ(C),   zn = fθ(B)

5. Choose the desired neighbor

The positive C resembles A in style while contradicting its claim. The negative B conveys a related idea in an academic parody. “Positive” and “negative” identify the desired stylistic ranking; they say nothing about truth, quality, or agreement.
This illustrative triplet encodes a style preference. In the StyleDistance paper, a style-changing negative can paraphrase the anchor or the positive; a negative need not always share the anchor’s content.

6. Compare the two distances

The solid line connects A to C, the desired style neighbor. The dashed line connects A to B, the semantically related text in a different style. Compare their squared Euclidean distances using both coordinate differences.
d²(zi, zj) = Σk(zik − zjk)²

7. A margin defines the target

Triplet loss asks C to be closer to A than B is by at least a margin α. The loss reaches zero once that inequality is satisfied. It neither demands identical embeddings for A and C nor requires an unlimited increase in the A–B distance.
L = max{0, d²(A,C) − d²(A,B) + α},   α = 0.1
This is the squared-distance triplet hinge objective described in the StyleDistance paper. The numerical distances here come from our schematic coordinates.

8. Change the encoder, not the points

Differentiate the loss through the shared encoder and update its trainable parameters. The published StyleDistance model adapts pretrained RoBERTa with LoRA, so the small low-rank updates are trained while the base weights remain frozen.
θtrainable ← θtrainable − η ∇θtrainableL
The published method freezes the base model and trains LoRA updates; the instructor’s linked notebook is a separate, unverified implementation.

9. Re-encode all four passages

Scroll to recompute the embeddings after the shared parameters change: A and C draw together while A and B separate. Even D, absent from this triplet, can move because it passes through the same encoder. Scroll back to restore the earlier geometry.
This reversible motion illustrates the intended training preference. It is not an optimizer trace, and D’s movement alone does not establish its correct stylistic ranking.

10. Repeat across varied examples

The target geometry makes A closer to C in style despite their opposite meanings. A useful model needs many comparisons across topics and registers, including examples that defeat shortcuts based on shared words or length. The published work combines synthetic style data with natural authorship data.
The published main checkpoint and its synthetic-only variant use different data mixtures. The displayed geometry is a teaching illustration for neither checkpoint.

11. Test on new passages

Reserve new passages and labeled comparisons before fitting the model, then compare rankings under the pretrained and adapted encoders. Include semantically related negatives, style-matched positives on unfamiliar topics, and examples with little shared vocabulary.
Held-out triplet accuracy: proportion with d(za, zp) < d(za, zn)
No held-out scores are supplied here. Any claim that fine-tuning helped requires actual encoder outputs and evaluation data.

12. Closeness does not imply agreement

A and B remain semantically related even when separated in style space. A and C can become stylistic neighbors while contradicting one another. Fine-tuning rewards the relationship chosen for the task; success on this example would not establish complete separation of style from meaning.
