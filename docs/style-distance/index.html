Teach an embedding model what “similar” means

A pretrained text encoder may bring passages together because they discuss similar things. This story uses the StyleDistance approach to show how fine-tuning can instead reward similar writing styles across different content.

Fine-tuning for writing style

Carry the argument with you

  • The training objective defines which relationships a representation should preserve.
  • A shared encoder processes every member of a triplet, and its parameter updates can change every text embedding.
  • Triplet loss compares an anchor–positive distance with an anchor–negative distance and a margin.
  • A schematic geometric improvement is not evidence of model performance; evaluate held-out texts and styles.

All four passages are neutral teaching examples. Every displayed coordinate, distance, and loss is calculated from a labeled two-dimensional schematic, not from a trained language model. The published StyleDistance methodology supplies the learning idea; the instructor’s linked training notebook has not been inspected.

Read the complete lesson

1. A vector has a purpose

Word2vec learns word vectors through a prediction task, and transformers produce contextual representations of text. An existing encoder therefore brings a useful starting representation, but the similarity it expresses may differ from the similarity we want to measure.

2. Keep content and style separate

These four teaching passages vary two things: what they discuss and how they are written. A budget sentence and a bridge sentence differ in content; a plain sentence and a bureaucratic paraphrase differ in style.
The neutral passages are new teaching examples, not the StyleDistance training data. Circles identify the budget topic, squares identify the bridge topic, and each point retains its letter.

3. Start from a pretrained encoder

Suppose the starting representation primarily brings texts with similar content together. That can be useful for semantic search, but our task is to recognize writing style across different subjects.

4. Use one encoder three times

The anchor, positive, and negative passages each pass through the same encoder with shared parameters θ. This produces three vectors whose distances can enter one training objective.
za = fθ(a),   zp = fθ(p),   zn = fθ(n)

5. Choose a triplet

Use B as the anchor and D as a positive example: both are bureaucratic, although they discuss different things. Use A as the negative example: it expresses the anchor’s content in a different style, so content alone cannot solve this comparison.
This is one illustrative triplet construction. In the paper, a style-changing negative can paraphrase the anchor or the positive; a negative need not always share the anchor’s content.

6. Compare the two distances

The solid line connects the anchor to its positive and the dashed line connects it to its negative. For this demonstration we compare squared Euclidean distances, so both coordinate differences enter the calculation.
d²(zi, zj) = Σk(zik − zjk)²

7. A margin defines the target

Triplet loss asks the positive to be closer to the anchor than the negative by at least a margin α. The loss becomes zero once that inequality is satisfied; zero loss does not require identical embeddings for texts with the same style.
L = max{0, d²(za, zp) − d²(za, zn) + α},   α = 0.1
This is the squared-distance triplet hinge objective described in the StyleDistance paper. The numerical distances here come from our schematic coordinates.

8. Change the encoder, not the points

Differentiate the loss through the shared encoder and update its trainable parameters. The published StyleDistance model adapts pretrained RoBERTa with LoRA, so the small low-rank updates are trained while the base weights remain frozen.
θtrainable ← θtrainable − η ∇θtrainableL
The published method freezes the base model and trains LoRA updates; the instructor’s linked notebook is a separate, unverified implementation.

9. Re-encode all four passages

Apply the updated encoder again to obtain new vectors. Because the parameters are shared, even passage C, which did not appear in this triplet, can receive a different embedding.

10. Repeat across varied examples

A useful style representation needs comparisons spanning many topics and styles, rather than memorizing this small set. The published work combines synthetic style data with natural authorship data; our four passages only expose the logic of one training signal.
The published main checkpoint and its synthetic-only variant use different data mixtures. The displayed geometry is a teaching illustration for neither checkpoint.

11. Test on new passages

Reserve new passages and labeled comparisons before fitting the model, then compare their rankings under the pretrained and adapted encoders. Include content-matched negatives and style-matched positives across unfamiliar topics, rather than evaluating on the triplets used for training.
Held-out triplet accuracy: proportion with d(za, zp) < d(za, zn)
No held-out scores are supplied here. Any claim that fine-tuning helped requires actual encoder outputs and evaluation data.

12. Similarity depends on the task

Fine-tuning changes the representation toward the relationships rewarded by its objective. A style-sensitive embedding may be useful for studying writing while being a poor choice for semantic retrieval, and success on familiar styles does not establish generalization or complete separation of style from content.
