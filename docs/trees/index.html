01 / 08 · ONE PREDICTOR

One split, two predictions

A regression tree asks a sequence of questions about the inputs. With one predictor x, the first question might be whether x ≤ −0.5.

Each answer leads to a leaf, which returns one constant prediction. Here the left leaf predicts 0 and the right leaf predicts 1. Draw the prediction against x, and the tree becomes a step function.

The thresholds and leaf values in this explainer are chosen to make the geometry clear. We are not fitting these trees to a dataset.

02 / 08 · RECURSIVE PARTITIONING

Split a leaf, not the whole line

To grow the tree, choose one existing leaf and ask another question there. We split the right leaf at x = 0.75, assigning predictions 1 and 3 to its two children.

The original left leaf is untouched. An input reaches the second question only after passing the first one.

This is recursive partitioning: repeatedly divide a region that an earlier split has already created. With one predictor, the resulting leaves are intervals.

03 / 08 · ADDING FUNCTIONS

Add another tree

We can construct the same step function another way. Keep the first tree, T₁, and add a second tree, T₂, that contributes 0 up to 0.75 and 2 above it.

F(x) = T₁(x) + T₂(x).

At each input, follow both trees to their leaves and add the two numbers. Their cutpoints divide the line into intervals on which the sum is constant.

The result is again 0, then 1, then 3. Growing one tree and adding two smaller trees have produced the same predictions here, through different constructions.

Use the contribution control to add T₂ gradually. During the replay, λ runs from 0 to 1 in F = T₁ + λT₂.

04 / 08 · TWO PREDICTORS

Now the inputs form a plane

With two predictors, each possible input is a point (x₁, x₂). The horizontal axis is x₁; the vertical axis is x₂. Predictions are the numbers inside the regions.

A split at x₁ = 0 draws a vertical boundary. Every point to its left receives one leaf prediction, and every point to its right receives the other.

A one-split tree is often called a stump. This stump depends on x₁ alone: moving up or down within either half does not change its prediction.

05 / 08 · CONDITIONAL SPLITS

The next boundary is local

Consider another tree, U. First split at x₁ = 0, then split the right child at x₂ = 0. The horizontal boundary belongs only to the right half of the plane.

Give the upper-right leaf a prediction of 2 and the other leaves a prediction of 0. The tree now contributes 2 only when both predictors are positive.

U(x₁, x₂) = 2 · 1{x₁ > 0, x₂ > 0}.

The indicator, 1{…}, equals 1 when its condition is true and 0 otherwise. The tree has three leaves even though two of them share a prediction.

06 / 08 · OVERLAYING PARTITIONS

Two stumps, four regions

Return to two separate stumps. Let T₁ contribute 1 when x₁ > 0, and let T₂ contribute 2 when x₂ > 0.

To understand their sum, overlay their partitions. Each resulting region is an intersection of one leaf from each tree. Its prediction is the sum of those leaves’ values.

The four regions predict 0, 1, 2, and 3. Move the same input across the three maps to see which two leaves supply its prediction.

Colors use a shared prediction scale. Numbers are added; colors are not mixed. In general, some boundaries can disappear from the prediction function if their jumps cancel.

07 / 08 · WHAT DEPTH ADDS

Additive in trees—and in predictors?

Our sum of two stumps has four regions, but no interaction. Crossing x₁ = 0 always adds 1, whether x₂ is positive or negative. Each predictor contributes separately.

Now add the tree U with the conditional split. Only the upper-right prediction rises, from 3 to 5. Crossing x₁ = 0 then adds 3 above the horizontal boundary and 1 below it. The effect of one predictor depends on the other.

A sum of stumps is additive in predictors: c + ∑j gj(xj). A sum of deeper trees can include interactions. Both are additive in trees.

We are discussing numerical regression predictions. A nonlinear transformation, such as converting a classification score to a probability, changes the scale on which additivity holds.

08 / 08 · FROM TREES TO BOOSTING

How should we choose the next tree?

A single tree refines its own partition. An ensemble combines the partitions of its constituent trees, adding their predictions at every input.

A finite sum of these constant-leaf, axis-aligned trees can itself be represented by a larger tree. The distinction lies in how we construct and constrain the model, including the depth of each added tree and the size of its contribution.

We can build a function by adding small trees. How should we choose the next one?

The next explainer changes coordinate systems. Here, the axes described inputs. In the boosting story, each axis will represent a model’s prediction for one training observation.

The construction and the function

The partition created by an ensemble is the common refinement of its trees’ leaf regions: all the nonempty intersections. A tree’s local boundary stays local in this overlay. The sum is constant within each intersection, although adjacent regions can have the same prediction.

Representing that sum as one tree may require a much larger tree. Fitting one large tree and sequentially fitting many small ones are different learning procedures, even when their final prediction functions can be represented in either form.

Further reading
