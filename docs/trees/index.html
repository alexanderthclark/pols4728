Start by predicting the mean delivery time, 12 minutes, at every distance. The mean squared error is 18.75 minutes squared.

Distance is the predictor, delivery time is the outcome, and each point is one observation. We use squared-error loss and a learning rate of 1 throughout this worked example.

02 / 13 · FIT THE FIRST CORRECTION

Fit a tree to the residuals

A residual is the observed time minus the current prediction. From our 12-minute starting point, the residuals are −6, −6, −1, −1, −1, 5, 5, and 5 minutes.

Fit a one-split regression tree—a stump—to those residuals. The best split among the midpoints between observed distances is 8 km. Each leaf predicts its mean residual: −3 minutes on the left and +5 on the right.

Scroll to measure the errors, form the residuals, split them, and fit the two leaf means. Scrolling back reverses the sequence; the controls let you inspect or replay an action. Open the split comparison to see why 8 km gives the lowest fitting error.

The upper plot shows residuals and their fitted correction. The lower plot shows original delivery times and the accumulated prediction. Each plot keeps its axes fixed across rounds.

03 / 13 · ADD THE FIRST TREE

Add the correction to the current prediction

The first tree, h₁, predicts a change in minutes. Add that change to the 12-minute baseline:

F₁(x) = 12 + h₁(x).

As you scroll, the correction attaches to the old prediction, moves it, and reveals the remaining errors. The left level moves down 3 minutes while the right level moves up 5.

The result is 12 − 3 = 9 minutes up to 8 km and 12 + 5 = 17 above it. Training mean squared error falls from 18.75 to 3.75. The longer deliveries are fitted exactly; the shorter ones still leave errors to correct.

04 / 13 · FIT WHAT REMAINS

Recompute the residuals, then fit again

Subtract the new prediction from each observed time. The residuals are now −3, −3, 2, 2, 2, 0, 0, and 0 minutes. These are the targets for the next tree.

The best stump now splits at 3.5 km. Its left leaf predicts −3. Its right leaf predicts +1, the mean of the six residuals to the right.

Follow the residuals changing in the upper plot while the current prediction stays fixed below. Then split and fit the new residuals. The second tree, h₂, chooses a different cutpoint because what remains to be learned has changed.

05 / 13 · ADD THE SECOND TREE

Keep the first tree and add the second

Add h₂ to the current prediction at every distance:

F₂(x) = 12 + h₁(x) + h₂(x).

The shortest deliveries move from 9 to 6 minutes. Both intervals to the right of 3.5 km rise together by 1: from 9 to 10 in the middle, and from 17 to 18 above 8 km. They share the second tree’s right leaf even though the first tree gave them different predictions.

In the final action, inspect the remaining errors. The middle group improves, but the long deliveries now overshoot by 1 minute. Together with the improvement on the left, the changes reduce training mean squared error from 3.75 to 0.75.

06 / 13 · TWO TREES, ONE FUNCTION

Two stumps, three plateaus

Each fitted tree has just one cutpoint and two leaves. Their sum keeps both cutpoints, 3.5 and 8 km, so it has three intervals with predictions 6, 10, and 18 minutes.

As you scroll, the guide follows one input through both trees. Use “Inspect distance” to examine a particular input, or “Trace the sum” to replay the sweep. At 6 km, the two contributions are −3 and +1, giving 12 − 3 + 1 = 10 minutes. Watch which contribution changes when the cursor crosses each cutpoint.

With one predictor, two distinct cutpoints make three intervals. With two predictors, a new split can cross an earlier boundary. That changes the comparison between growing one tree and adding another.

These are training errors for a small illustrative dataset. Better predictions on new deliveries would need to be checked separately.

07 / 13 · TWO PREDICTORS

Put the inputs in a plane

Now set the delivery example aside and examine the geometry with two predictors. Each input is a point (x₁, x₂). The horizontal axis is x₁ and the vertical axis is x₂. The number in each region is its prediction.

Our first split asks whether x₁ ≤ 0. It divides the entire plane into two regions: the left leaf predicts 0 and the right leaf predicts 1.

We choose these leaf values to isolate the geometry. Suppose we have a budget of one more split. Where can that split go?

08 / 13 · THE SAME TWO SPLITS

Two splits: three regions or four

In one tree, the second split must divide an existing leaf. Split its right leaf at x₂ = 0, and there are three regions. The horizontal boundary stops at the first split; the whole left half still predicts 0.

With two stumps, the second split starts a separate tree. That tree splits on x₂ across the entire plane. Add its predictions to the first stump, and the crossing boundaries create four regions.

Both constructions use two split nodes in total. The difference is where the second split acts: inside one leaf, or in a new tree evaluated throughout the input space.

Watch the upper-left region. It stays joined to the lower-left region in the single tree, but receives a different prediction in the sum.

09 / 13 · ADDING PREDICTIONS

Why does adding a stump cross the first split?

The first stump, T₁, contributes 1 when x₁ > 0. The second, T₂, contributes 2 when x₂ > 0. Otherwise each contributes 0.

F(x₁, x₂) = T₁(x₁) + T₂(x₂).

Every input passes through both trees. The second stump therefore adds 2 to the upper half on both sides of the first boundary. It is a separate function, not a new branch appended to the first tree.

Move an input across the maps to trace the two leaf values and their sum. The contribution control gradually adds T₂.

This is the geometry of the sums of trees used in additive boosting. We have chosen the trees to illustrate it; this is not a particular boosting fitting step.

10 / 13 · MATCHING THE PARTITION

One tree needs a third split

A single tree can reproduce all four regions. After splitting on x₁, split both children on x₂.

There are now three split nodes: the first question, the question inside its left child, and the question inside its right child. The repeated test x₂ ≤ 0 counts twice because it appears at two different nodes.

Assign the leaves 0, 1, 2, and 3, and this larger tree makes exactly the same predictions as the two stumps. The sum does not require a fundamentally new kind of function; it constructs this partition using fewer split nodes.

11 / 13 · WHAT THE FOUR VALUES CAN DO

Four regions, but not four free values

The larger single tree can assign its four leaf predictions independently. The sum of the two stumps cannot: the same contribution from x₁ applies above and below the horizontal boundary.

Crossing x₁ = 0 adds 1 at either height. Its effect does not depend on x₂, so these four regions do not imply an interaction.

The upper-right value is fixed by the other three: lower right + upper left − lower left. Here that is 1 + 2 − 0 = 3.

More regions and more freedom in their predictions are different things. The crossing partition is richer than either stump’s partition, while the sum still obeys an additive constraint.

12 / 13 · WHAT DEPTH ADDS

A conditional split can add an interaction

Consider a tree U that first splits on x₁, then splits only the right child on x₂. Set its upper-right leaf to 2 and its other leaves to 0. It contributes 2 only when both predictors are positive.

Add U to our two stumps. Only the upper-right prediction rises, from 3 to 5. Crossing x₁ = 0 then adds 3 above the horizontal boundary and 1 below it. The effect of one predictor depends on the other.

A sum of stumps is additive in predictors: c + ∑j gj(xj). A sum of deeper trees can include interactions. Both are additive in trees.

We are discussing numerical regression predictions. A nonlinear transformation, such as converting a classification score to a probability, changes the scale on which additivity holds.

13 / 13 · FROM TREES TO BOOSTING

How should we choose the next tree?

A single tree refines its own partition. An ensemble combines the partitions of its constituent trees, adding their predictions at every input.

We have seen how adding small trees builds a function. Boosting supplies a way to choose each new tree using what the current ensemble has left to learn.

The construction and the function

More generally, an ensemble overlays its trees’ leaf regions. The resulting partition contains all their nonempty intersections. Local boundaries stay local, and the prediction is constant within each intersection. Adjacent regions can share a prediction; jumps from different trees can also cancel.

A finite sum of constant-leaf, axis-aligned trees can be represented by a larger single tree. Fitting that tree and sequentially fitting many small trees remain different learning procedures, with different constraints.

What changes with just one predictor?

On a line, two distinct cutpoints make three intervals in either construction. For the delivery example, a single tree can split at 8 km and then split its left leaf at 3.5 km, assigning predictions 6, 10, and 18.

Our two fitted stumps produce those same predictions when their leaf values are added to the 12-minute baseline. Two different constructions can therefore represent the same final function.

The later comparison uses two predictors because a split on a different predictor can cross the first boundary. A second cutpoint on the same line cannot create that crossing.
