01 / A shallow network

A linear equation, drawn as a network.

ŷ = wx + b = x + 0.5

The arrow from x has weight w = 1. The faint constant-1 node adds the intercept b = 0.5; it is not a second observed feature.

Bias connections disappear from the drawing for clarity. The intercept remains part of the equation and the model.

A ReLU clips the negative part.

ReLU keeps positive inputs and clips negative inputs to zero, creating a joint at zero.

ReLU(x) = max(0, x)

Move the joint. Change the slope.

First move the joint with t. Then change a to steepen the slope and turn it downward.

a ReLU(x − t)

The contribution stays zero before t. Faint dashed curves show comparison examples.

One neuron starts the climb.

The first neuron activates at −1, adding +1 to the slope.

f(x) = ReLU(x + 1)

A second neuron turns it downward.

The second activates at zero and adds −2 to the slope: +1 − 2 = −1. Both neurons keep contributing.

f(x) = ReLU(x + 1) − 2 ReLU(x)

A third neuron flattens it again.

The third activates at +1 and adds +1, returning the total slope to zero.

f(x) = ReLU(x + 1)− 2 ReLU(x) + ReLU(x − 1)

That is a shallow neural network.

One hidden layer computes the ReLUs. A linear output adds their weighted activations and a bias.

f(x) = c + ∑j aj ReLU(wjx + bj)

02 / What width changes

One neuron, one place to bend.

The width is the number of neurons in our single hidden layer. One neuron can introduce at most one joint, dividing the input interval into two linear pieces.

A second neuron adds another joint.

Add a second neuron with a different threshold. The output sums both hinges: two joints, three linear pieces.

Three neurons, four straight pieces.

A third neuron adds another slope change. All three receive the same scalar input in parallel, producing three joints and four linear pieces.

A wider layer allows more joints.

The layer widens from three neurons to six, giving this example six joints and seven linear pieces.

In general, width m allows at most m joints and m + 1 pieces. Inactive units, zero weights, coincident joints, or cancellation can reduce the count.

03 / Approximation by adding joints

Approximate a smooth target.

Approximate sin(x) on [0, 2π] with the same shallow architecture. Six hidden neurons supply the initial slope and five joints, joining selected points with straight segments.

Zoom into the gap.

Between joints, the straight segment misses the curved target. The vertical gap is the error at this input.

Add a neuron and a joint

Add a joint on the sine curve at the marked input. One new neuron, plus updated existing output weights, replaces one segment with two and makes the error zero here.

Keep adding places to bend.

The layer widens from seven neurons to twenty-six. Shorter segments approach the sine curve; the result looks smooth but remains piecewise linear.

Approximate a continuous curve.

A continuous function on a closed, bounded interval can be approximated uniformly as closely as we want by a sufficiently wide, one-hidden-layer ReLU network. Continuity lets sufficiently fine straight segments stay close throughout the interval.

This guarantees that suitable weights exist. It does not guarantee that training finds them, specify data requirements, or justify predictions outside the interval.

Nathaniel Beck, Gary King, and Langche Zeng. 2000. “Improving Quantitative Studies of International Conflict: A Conjecture.” American Political Science Review 94(1): 21–35, Equation 3.

From logistic regression to a neural network.

Both models use a Bernoulli outcome. Logistic regression applies the sigmoid directly to an affine predictor:

πi=sigmoid⁡(Xiβ)\pi_i=\operatorname{sigmoid}(X_i\beta)πi​=sigmoid(Xi​β)

The network combines several sigmoid features before the final sigmoid:

πi=sigmoid⁡ ⁣[γ0+∑j=1Mγjsigmoid⁡(Xiβ(j))]\pi_i=\operatorname{sigmoid}\!\left[\gamma_0+\sum_{j=1}^{M}\gamma_j\operatorname{sigmoid}(X_i\beta_{(j)})\right]πi​=sigmoid[γ0​+j=1∑M​γj​sigmoid(Xi​β(j)​)]

With one input, logistic regression gives a monotone probability curve; with two inputs, its classification boundary is a straight line. A sigmoid network can instead express a hump and a curved, enclosed boundary. The illustrative weights are chosen to show capacity, and the observations stay fixed throughout the comparison. The following scenes show how adding hidden neurons changes the probability curve.

More neurons allow more complicated shapes.

Start with one hidden sigmoid, which gives a rising probability curve. Keep it fixed and introduce a second sigmoid centered at 0.4. Give its output a negative weight. As the second activation increases, its negative contribution pulls the score down. The resulting probability rises and then falls.

p^(x)=σ ⁣(−3+6h1(x)−6h2(x))\widehat p(x)=\sigma\!\left(-3+6h_1(x)-6h_2(x)\right)p​(x)=σ(−3+6h1​(x)−6h2​(x))

Another pair creates another peak.

Add a positive contribution centered at 0.6, then a negative contribution centered at 0.8. Their weighted sum creates a second high-probability region. More neurons permit more shapes; they do not require more complicated fitted curves or guarantee better prediction.

All four hidden sigmoids have slope parameter 30. The output bias is −3 and the output weights are 6, −6, 6, and −6. Existing parameters stay fixed as each new contribution appears.

The hidden features form a representation.

Collect the hidden activations into a vector h(x)\mathbf h(\mathbf x)h(x), the input’s representation. The final affine score and sigmoid form a classification head. Once the representation is fixed, that head is logistic regression on the hidden features.

p^(x)=σ(ϕ0+ϕ⊤h(x))\widehat p(\mathbf x)=\sigma(\phi_0+\boldsymbol\phi^\top\mathbf h(\mathbf x))p​(x)=σ(ϕ0​+ϕ⊤h(x))

So far we chose the parameters to demonstrate possible functions. Fitting a network learns the representation and the head jointly. Next we examine an actual fitted binary network.

05 / Learned representations

Two curved classes, two hidden neurons.

Our binary example has two inputs and two adjacent curved groups. No straight line separates the classes in the original coordinates. We fit a shallow network with two ReLU hidden neurons and one sigmoid output. We have changed the hidden activation from the historical sigmoid example; the representation-plus-head interpretation is the same.

hj(x)=ReLU⁡(θj0+θj1x1+θj2x2),j=1,2h_j(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{ReLU}(\theta_{j0}+\theta_{j1}x_1+\theta_{j2}x_2),\quad j=1,2hj​(x)=ReLU(θj0​+θj1​x1​+θj2​x2​),j=1,2

This is a new binary fit inspired by the course’s curved three-class example. It is not the original three-class fitted network. The displayed boundary is calculated from this binary network’s actual fitted weights.

One observation gets two new coordinates.

Follow the marked observation through the two hidden neurons. Each takes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and applies ReLU. The resulting pair of activations is this observation’s location in representation space.

(x1,x2)⟼(h1(x),h2(x))(x_1,x_2)\longmapsto(h_1(\mathbf x),h_2(\mathbf x))(x1​,x2​)⟼(h1​(x),h2​(x))

Move the observations into representation space.

Every point now moves from its input coordinates to its actual hidden activations, preserving its identity and class. The network has exactly two hidden neurons, so we can plot the entire representation directly, without dimensionality reduction.

The movement illustrates a change of coordinates for an already fitted network. It is not an animation of training. The classifier’s straight boundary remains hidden until the movement is complete.

Now one straight line separates the classes.

Reveal the fitted classification head. Its probability equals 0.5 where its affine score is zero, giving a straight decision boundary in the learned coordinates.

p^=σ(ϕ0+ϕ1h1+ϕ2h2)\widehat p=\sigma(\phi_0+\phi_1h_1+\phi_2h_2)p​=σ(ϕ0​+ϕ1​h1​+ϕ2​h2​)

p^=12  ⟺  ϕ0+ϕ1h1+ϕ2h2=0\widehat p=\tfrac12\iff\phi_0+\phi_1h_1+\phi_2h_2=0p​=21​⟺ϕ0​+ϕ1​h1​+ϕ2​h2​=0

This head correctly classifies every training observation at the 0.5 cutoff. We use the network’s own head, not a newly fitted classifier. Its probabilities are not exactly zero or one.

A simple head on learned features.

The same fitted classifier has a nonlinear boundary in the original inputs and a straight boundary in the hidden representation. The hidden layer learned features that make these training observations linearly separable; the classification head is logistic regression on those features.

The representation and head were learned together from the labels. Perfect training classification does not imply perfect probability estimates or perfect predictions for new observations.
