Principal component analysis

PCA: a change of basis

The same observations, measured along new directions.

Start with x₁ = x₂ ↓

Scroll, use Previous and Next, or choose a lesson step.

01 · A simple example

Two variables with the same values

Begin with five observations: (−2, −2), (−1, −1), (0, 0), (1, 1), and (2, 2). Every point lies on the line x₁ = x₂. Knowing either coordinate tells us the other.

We have recorded two variables, but these observations vary along only one direction. PCA asks which directions describe the variation in the data.

These variables already have mean zero. Throughout the lesson, X contains centered observations in its rows. The examples are constructed data, with no outcome variable.

02 · Change of basis

Turn the axes, keep the points

The familiar coordinate directions are e₁ = (1, 0)ᵀ and e₂ = (0, 1)ᵀ. We can describe the same points using a different pair of perpendicular unit vectors, u and v.

Rotate the frame with the angle control. The observations stay where they are in the original plot; the directions along which we measure them change. This is a change of basis.

u = (cos θ, sin θ)ᵀ
v = (−sin θ, cos θ)ᵀ

Both arrows have length one. The same physical distance represents one unit along either axis.

03 · A new coordinate

Measure a point along u

Follow the observation at (2, 2), marked with a ring. Drop a perpendicular to the line through u. Its signed distance along that line is its new coordinate, or score.

zi=xi⊤u=xi1u1+xi2u2

The score is one number. The projected point in the original plot is zᵢu, a vector with two entries. Rotate to 45°: the ringed point is already on the new axis, and its score is 2√2.

At 30°, z = 2 cos 30° + 2 sin 30° ≈ 2.73.

04 · All the observations

Xu stacks the new coordinates

Apply that same dot product to every row of X. Matrix multiplication collects the results into one column:

Xu=[x1⊤u⋮xn⊤u]

X is n × 2, u is 2 × 1, and Xu is n × 1. Here, five observations give five scores. A direction in variable space produces a column of measurements across observations.

05 · Measure the spread

Some directions reveal more variation

Looking along the horizontal axis gives scores −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, with sample variance 2.5. Looking along the line gives scores √2 times as large, with variance 5. Looking perpendicular to the line gives five zeros.

Move the angle control and watch the score strip. The quantity Var(Xu) is the sample variance of this column of scores across the observations. Centered data make their mean zero:

Var(Xu)=∑i=1nzi2n−1

Try 0°, 45°, and 135°. The strip uses a fixed scale, so a wider spread means a larger variance.

06 · The covariance matrix

The same variance, written with S

The sum of squared scores is (Xu)ᵀ(Xu). Rearrange the multiplication and define the sample covariance matrix S = XᵀX/(n − 1):

Var(Xu)=(Xu)⊤(Xu)n−1=u⊤Su

For our five points, the two variances and their covariance are all 2.5. Substituting u = (u₁, u₂)ᵀ gives:

Var(Xu) = 2.5u₁² + 5u₁u₂ + 2.5u₂²
= 2.5(u₁ + u₂)²

The term involving both entries of u comes from covariance. PCA uses how the variables move together, as well as their individual variances.

07 · Principal directions

Choose u to maximize Var(Xu)

We compare directions of the same length: uᵀu = 1. Otherwise doubling u would double every score and quadruple its variance without finding a better direction.

maximize uᵀSu subject to uᵀu = 1

The maximizing direction is an eigenvector of S with the largest eigenvalue. In this example, u₁ = (1, 1)ᵀ/√2 and Su₁ = 5u₁. Its score variance is 5. The perpendicular direction u₂ = (−1, 1)ᵀ/√2 has variance 0.

These are the first and second principal directions. Their score columns are the principal components. Reversing an arrow changes the signs of the scores, but leaves the variance unchanged.

08 · Keep both coordinates

The same points in the new basis

Put the two principal directions into the columns of U = [u₁ u₂]. Multiplying by this matrix gives both new coordinates at once:

Z = XU
X = ZUᵀ

The paired plots use equal units. The diagonal line becomes a horizontal line because its second coordinate is zero. The distances between observations stay the same.

Keeping the full orthonormal basis preserves the data. Here, even the first score alone describes every observation, because the second score is always zero.

09 · A more complicated 2D example

Now give the line some thickness

Replace the five points with a cloud of 24 centered observations. The variables still tend to move together, but now knowing one does not determine the other.

S = 5445

The principal directions still lie at 45° and 135°. Their score variances are now 9 and 1. In the principal basis, the score covariance is zero; the two diagonal entries add to the same total variance, 10.

For a general angle, Var(Xu) = 5 + 4 sin(2θ). Rotate the frame to see variation shift between coordinates. Zero covariance does not establish independence.

10 · Dimension reduction

Dropping a coordinate loses information

Keep the first score and set the second to zero. Reconstruct each observation as x̂ᵢ = zᵢ₁u₁. The reconstructed points lie on the first principal axis; the short segments show the differences from the original observations.

At the principal basis, retaining one component keeps 9/(9 + 1) = 90% of the total sample variance. The remaining 10% describes variation perpendicular to that axis.

Rotate the retained direction. The direction with the greatest score variance also gives the smallest sum of squared perpendicular reconstruction errors. The basis change itself preserved the data; discarding a coordinate introduces the loss.

Covariance PCA depends on the variables’ units. These examples use comparable scales. Centering subtracts column means; standardizing also divides by column standard deviations and can change the principal directions.

11 · A 3D example

Find one direction first

Now give the 24 observations three coordinates. Begin with the same question as before: which unit direction gives the largest Var(Xu)?

The first principal direction follows the cloud’s long axis: u1=(1, 1, 1)⊤/3. Its scores z₁ = Xu₁ have variance 9, out of total variance 10.25.

This finds PC1. To find the next direction, we can take away the part PC1 already describes and inspect what remains.

Turn the camera to inspect the cloud. This changes the drawing, while the direction and its score variance stay fixed.

12 · Remove the first component

Subtract each point’s projection onto u₁

PC1 describes a vector zᵢ₁u₁ for each observation. Subtract that vector to obtain a residual:

rᵢ = xᵢ − zᵢ₁u₁
R = X − z₁u₁ᵀ

Move the removal control from 0% to 100%. Each point travels parallel to u₁, until its coordinate along that direction is zero. Hollow circles mark the original observations.

At 100%, every residual satisfies rᵢᵀu₁ = 0. All the remaining variation lies in the shaded plane through the origin, perpendicular to PC1. We have reduced the search for the next direction to a 2D problem.

The control removes a fraction t of each PC1 score. Its remaining variance is 9(1 − t)²; at t = 1, the total residual variance is 1.25.

13 · A 2D problem again

Look straight at the remaining plane

These are the residual points after fully removing PC1, viewed face on. The axes a and b form a reference basis within this plane. Both are perpendicular to u₁; neither was chosen to maximize variance.

Rotate a unit direction w within the plane, and compare the variance of Rw. For any such direction, u₁ᵀw = 0, so removing PC1 does not change its scores:

Rw = Xw − z₁(u₁ᵀw) = Xw

The problem is the same one we solved in 2D: find the direction with the greatest score variance. The total variance available in this plane is 1.25.

The angle φ is measured from a within this plane. It is a new angle, separate from the earlier 2D example and from the 3D camera.

14 · The next principal component

The best remaining direction is PC2

Here the maximum occurs at φ = −30°. It gives u2=(1, −1, 0)⊤/2, with score variance 1. This is the second principal direction of the original data: the best direction perpendicular to u₁.

PC2 describes 1/1.25 = 80% of the residual variance. Relative to the original total, it contributes 1/10.25 ≈ 9.8%. These percentages have different denominators.

Subtract the PC2 contribution as well, and the residual points lie on a line. Its direction is u3=(1, 1, −2)⊤/6, with score variance 0.25. Together, the three perpendicular directions form the full basis.

Try the PC3 preset to see the smallest variance in this plane. Sequential removal explains the perpendicularity constraints; it is one way to understand the components.

15 · Return to the original data

Add the first two contributions back

To reconstruct an original observation with two components, add its PC1 and PC2 contributions:

x̂ᵢ = zᵢ₁u₁ + zᵢ₂u₂

These reconstructed points occupy the plane spanned by u₁ and u₂. This is a different plane from the residual plane: it includes PC1 and omits PC3.

The first two components retain (9 + 1)/10.25 ≈ 97.6% of the original total variance. The short segments show the omitted PC3 contribution.

Change the number of retained components: one gives a line, two give a plane, and all three recover the original observations.

16 · Component contributions

Each component contributes one piece

Select a component to see its contribution to every observation. Each piece lies along one principal direction; the observations differ only in their scores along that direction.

X = z₁u₁ᵀ + z₂u₂ᵀ + z₃u₃ᵀ

A column of scores multiplied by a row containing its direction gives a matrix with the same shape as X. Each of these matrices has rank one.

Subtracting PC1 removed the first piece. Keeping PC1 and PC2 adds the first two pieces. Adding all three recovers the complete data matrix. This provides a bridge to the singular value decomposition.

17 · SVD intuition

Separate each score pattern from its size

The singular value decomposition writes the same centered data as X = PDUᵀ. Here U still contains the three principal directions. Multiplying by U gives the scores we already know:

Z = XU = PD
zⱼ = dⱼpⱼ

The column pⱼ records a pattern across the 24 observations, scaled to length one. The singular value dⱼ restores the score column’s length. Select a component and compare the two plots; their vertical scales are labelled separately.

Because the scores are centered, their sample variance is their squared length divided by n − 1:

Var(Xuⱼ) = dⱼ²/(n − 1)

For PC1, d1=23×9≈14.39, so its variance is 9. Each SVD piece dⱼpⱼuⱼᵀ is the same contribution zⱼuⱼᵀ seen in the previous step.

P is 24 × 3, D is 3 × 3 and diagonal, and U is 3 × 3. The columns of P are perpendicular unit patterns across observations; the columns of U are perpendicular unit directions among variables. Many texts call the right singular vector matrix V; we keep U to match our change-of-basis notation.

18 · Explore

Directions, scores, and retained information

Return to the exact line, the thicker 2D cloud, or the 3D cloud. Compare the original observations, their new coordinates, and their reconstructions.

For the exact line, find a direction with zero score variance. For the thicker cloud, find the angle that retains the most variation in one coordinate. For the 3D cloud, compare retaining a line, a plane, and the whole basis.

The calculation is the same throughout: Xu measures every observation along one unit direction, and uᵀSu measures the variance of those scores.
