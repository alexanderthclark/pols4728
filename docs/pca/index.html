Principal component analysis

PCA: a change of basis

The same observations, measured along new directions.

Start ↓

01 · The first principal component

Find the direction of greatest variance

For centered data X, choose the first principal direction:

u1≔argmax‖u‖=1Var(Xu)=argmax‖u‖=1‖Xu‖

02 · Read one score

One point, one score

Choose u=(1,0)⊤. Follow the ringed observation xi onto the horizontal axis. Its score is the signed distance from the origin to its projection; points on the other side of the origin have negative scores.

zi=xi⊤u

03 · The horizontal direction

Collect the scores in Xu

Xu=x1 collects one score per observation. For centered data, variance is proportional to the squared length of this column:

Var(Xu)=‖Xu‖2n−1

04 · The vertical direction

Next, choose u=(0,1)⊤

Projecting onto the vertical axis selects Xu=x2. Here x1=x2, so this gives the same score variance as the horizontal direction. Could another unit direction give more?

05 · The diagonal direction

The 45° direction gives more variance

Choose u=(1,1)⊤/2. The projections now reach farther from the origin, giving twice the variance along either original axis. All points already lie on this line, so no other unit direction can give greater variance. This is PC1.

06 · Change of basis

Add a perpendicular direction

Keep PC1 and add a perpendicular unit direction. Together they form a new basis: each observation has one score along each direction.

07 · Keep both coordinates

The same points in the new basis

Put the two directions in the columns of U. The new coordinates describe the same observations, with every distance preserved. Here the second coordinate is zero for everyone.

Z=XU

08 · A more complicated 2D example

The Less Extreme Case

The variables still tend to move together, but now the cloud has width. PC1 follows its long direction; the perpendicular direction also contains variation.

09 · The cloud in a new basis

The same cloud in the new basis

The basis change preserves the cloud’s shape and total variance. In the principal coordinates, the greatest variation is horizontal, but the vertical spread no longer disappears.

Z=XU

10 · Dimension reduction

Dropping a coordinate loses information

Hold the principal basis fixed and set the second score to zero. Each point moves onto PC1. The segments show the information lost when we reconstruct it from only its first score.

x^i=zi1u1

11 · A 3D example

Find one direction first

Now each observation has three coordinates. The question stays the same: which unit direction maximizes Var(Xu)? PC1 follows the cloud’s long axis. To find the next direction, remove the part PC1 describes.

12 · Remove the first component

Subtract each point’s projection onto u1

Each point travels parallel to PC1 until its score along that direction is zero. Collect the residuals in R. They lie in the plane perpendicular to PC1, where we will search for PC2.

ri=xi−zi1u1

13 · A 2D problem again

Look straight at the remaining plane

Watch the same residual points turn into view. Within this plane, choose a unit direction w and compare the variance of its scores Rw. We have the same problem as before, in two dimensions.

14 · The next principal component

The best remaining direction is PC2

Rotate within the residual plane to find the greatest score variance. The maximizing direction is PC2: the best direction in the original data subject to being perpendicular to PC1.

15 · One direction remains

Remove PC2 to reveal PC3

Subtract the PC2 contribution from these residuals. They collapse onto a line: PC3. Its direction is perpendicular to both PC1 and PC2. Together, the three directions form a full orthonormal basis.

16 · Return to the original data

Reconstruct from the first two components

Return to the original cloud and reconstruct each point from PC1 and PC2. This retained plane contains both directions; the earlier residual plane was perpendicular to PC1. The segments show the omitted PC3 contribution.

x^i=zi1u1+zi2u2

17 · Component contributions

Each component contributes one piece

Build the data one contribution at a time: PC1, then PC2, then PC3. Each adds a score column times a direction. The three pieces together recover every original observation.

X=z1u1⊤+z2u2⊤+z3u3⊤

18 · SVD intuition

Separate each score pattern from its size

For component j, start with a unit-length pattern pj across observations. The singular value dj scales it into the score column we already know. The pattern stays the same; its length changes.

zj=djpj

Directions, scores, and retained information

After the examples

What the simple cases teach us

Changing coordinates and reducing dimension are separate

Keeping every component is an orthogonal change of coordinates: a rotation, possibly with a reflection. Distances and total variance stay the same. Dimension reduction also drops score coordinates. Revisit the cloud in its new basis.

“Most spread” means greatest squared projection length

For centered X and unit u, PC1 maximizes ‖Xu‖2, the sum of squared projection distances from the mean. In the SVD picture, X sends that unit direction to the longest score vector. Changing the basis does not stretch the observations.

The retained subspace gives the best squared-error reconstruction

The first k principal directions span the k-dimensional subspace through the mean with the smallest total squared reconstruction error. On the exact line, one component loses nothing. In the thicker cloud, PC1 retains 9 of the total variance 10; dropping PC2 loses the remaining 1. Inspect the reconstruction errors.

Explained variance describes the whole dataset

Retaining 90% of variance means retaining 90% of the total centered sum of squares. It does not guarantee equally accurate reconstructions for every observation, or preserve every distance. Points that differ only along a discarded direction have identical retained scores.

Units and unusual observations matter

Centering subtracts means; it does not equalize variable scales. Changing a variable’s units can change the PCs. Standardizing changes the weight given to each variable. Squared distances also give extreme observations substantial influence.

Uncorrelated scores can still be related

Principal score columns are uncorrelated, but need not be independent: nonlinear relationships can remain. PCA finds linear subspaces, so a curved pattern may be represented poorly by just a few components.

Optional derivations

The mathematics behind the pictures

Open a derivation to connect the geometric story to covariance matrices, eigenvalues, and the singular value decomposition.

From score length to the covariance matrix

The sum of squared scores is (Xu)⊤(Xu). Rearrange the multiplication and define the sample covariance matrix S=X⊤Xn−1:

Var(Xu)=(Xu)⊤(Xu)n−1=u⊤Su

For our five points, the two variances and their covariance are all 2.5. Substituting u=(a,b)⊤ gives:

Var(Xu)=2.5a2+5ab+2.5b2=2.5(a+b)2

The term involving both entries of u comes from covariance. PCA uses how the variables move together, as well as their individual variances.

Principal directions, eigenvalues, and explained variance

We compare directions of the same length: u⊤u=1. Otherwise doubling u would double every score and quadruple its variance without finding a better direction.

maxu⊤u=1u⊤Su

The maximizing direction is an eigenvector of S with the largest eigenvalue. In this example, u1=(1,1)⊤/2 and Su1=5u1. Its score variance is 5. The perpendicular direction u2=(−1,1)⊤/2 has variance 0.

These are the first and second principal directions. Their score columns are the principal components. Reversing an arrow changes the signs of the scores, but leaves the variance unchanged.

In any number of variables, a unit eigenvector uj with covariance eigenvalue λj has score variance equal to that eigenvalue. The sum over a full orthonormal basis equals the sum of the original column variances:

Var(Xuj)=λjTotal variance=∑jλj=tr(S)

The explained variance ratio is λj∑kλk. It is an eigenvalue divided by the total, rather than the eigenvalue itself.

The covariance and variance formulas for the 2D cloud

The thicker cloud contains 24 centered observations with covariance matrix:

S=[5445]

The principal directions still lie at 45° and 135°. Their score variances are now 9 and 1. In the principal basis, the score covariance is zero; the two diagonal entries add to the same total variance, 10.

For a unit direction u=(cos⁡θ,sin⁡θ)⊤, the score variance is Var(Xu)=5+4sin⁡(2θ). The perpendicular direction has the complementary variance, so the sum stays 10.

Keeping both score columns is reversible: Z=XU and X=ZU⊤. Keeping only PC1 retains 9 of total variance 10. Centering subtracts column means; standardizing also divides by column standard deviations and may change the principal directions.

The 3D directions and the remaining-plane calculation

For this example the three principal directions are:

u1=(1,1,1)⊤/3u2=(1,−1,0)⊤/2u3=(1,1,−2)⊤/6

Their score variances are 9, 1, and 0.25, giving original total variance 10.25. Subtracting the first contribution gives R=X−z1u1⊤. Every residual satisfies ri⊤u1=0.

For any unit direction w in the residual plane, u1⊤w=0, so removing PC1 does not change its scores:

Rw=Xw−z1(u1⊤w)=Xw

The reference axes a and b form an orthonormal basis within the residual plane. The angle φ is measured from a there, independently of the 3D camera. PC2 occurs at φ=−30°.

PC2 describes 11.25=80% of the residual variance. Relative to the original total, it contributes 110.25≈9.8%. These percentages have different denominators.

After subtracting PC2 as well, the residuals lie along PC3. Sequential removal explains the perpendicularity constraints; it is one way to understand the components. Reconstructing the original observations with PC1 and PC2 instead retains 9+110.25≈97.6% of the original total variance.

The full SVD and its matrix dimensions

The singular value decomposition writes the same centered data as X=PDU⊤. Here U still contains the three principal directions. Multiplying by U gives the scores we already know:

Z=XU=PDzj=djpj

A score column times its direction gives a matrix of the same shape as X. Each component matrix has rank one. The SVD contribution djpjuj⊤ is the same piece as zjuj⊤.

Because the scores are centered, their sample variance is their squared length divided by n−1:

Var(Xuj)=dj2n−1

For PC1, d1=23×9≈14.39, so its variance is 9. Each SVD piece djpjuj⊤ is the same contribution zjuj⊤ seen in the previous step.

P is 24×3, D is 3×3 and diagonal, and U is 3×3. The columns of P are perpendicular unit patterns across observations; the columns of U are perpendicular unit directions among variables. Many texts call the right singular vector matrix V; we keep U to match our change-of-basis notation.
