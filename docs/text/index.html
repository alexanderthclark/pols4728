Choosing a representation

What should similarity mean?

A representation makes some differences visible and others hard to see. An authorship study, a document search, and a study of political language may need different features—even when they begin with the same texts.

RepresentationEach row representsWhat the coordinates record
Bag of wordsA documentCounts of vocabulary terms
TF–IDFA documentTerm counts weighted by document rarity
word2vecA word typeDense parameters learned by predicting local context

Both count representations discard word order within a document. Word2vec uses local windows during training, but its usual lookup still gives one vector per word type: the vector for bank is the same in a river story and a finance story. Contextual language models will change that last assumption.

Bag of words

Catch the words as they are spoken.

Imagine catching each word of a speech in a bag. Our Lincoln-like speaker says “government of the people, by the people, for the people.” Scroll to send each separate word from the speaker to the bag.

The fragment is from the Gettysburg Address. Scroll slowly to follow individual words into the bag.

Keep repetition. Let the order go.

After all ten words are caught, the bag contains “the” three times and “people” three times. There are six distinct terms. A bag-of-words representation retains these frequencies while setting aside their order in the speech.

Scroll backward to return words to the speaker. Next, apply the same counting idea to a collection of documents.

Begin with four short documents.

Suppose we want to compare what political texts discuss. These four constructed documents are small enough to inspect word by word. A computer needs a representation that lets the same operation compare every pair.

D1–D4 are teaching examples, not a sample of parliamentary speech.

Choose what counts as a word.

Here we lowercase the text and split it into alphabetic tokens, removing punctuation. “The” and “the” become the same term. These choices define what differences our analysis can see.

We keep common words and do not stem words. Other research questions may call for different choices.

Give every document the same coordinates.

Each column is a vocabulary term; each row is a document. A cell counts how often that term occurs. D1 has two instances of “budget” and three of “the.” Most cells in a realistic document–term matrix are zero.

Scroll back: the words return to their original positions. The count representation has one coordinate for every vocabulary term.

The bag loses word order.

“Senators support voters” and “Voters support senators” produce identical counts. Their meanings differ because the roles reverse. Counting can still be useful, but this representation cannot distinguish these sentences.

Bigrams can retain some local order, at the cost of a larger vocabulary.

Small words can reveal writing habits.

In the classic Federalist Papers study discussed in Text as Data, Mosteller and Wallace compared common-word frequencies in Hamilton’s and Madison’s writings. Their combined evidence favored Madison for the twelve disputed essays.

The chart shows approximate reported rates of “upon” in their comparison writings, including material outside the Federalist Papers. One word did not settle the case.

Compare Revelation with the Gospel of John.

Erwin and Oakes (2012) analyzed 500-word Greek samples using 75 common words from the Johannine corpus. Their correspondence analysis found distinct profiles for Revelation and the Gospel of John.

This is evidence relevant to shared authorship, not proof. Genre and editing also affect style; English counts would additionally reflect translators’ choices.

Choose features for the question.

For authorship, frequent function words can carry stylistic information. Removing them automatically could discard the signal. For topical comparison, we may instead want to emphasize terms that distinguish documents.

Compare rates or account for length when texts differ in size. A model cannot remove genre and topic differences simply by counting words.

TF–IDF

A frequent word need not be distinctive.

D1 contains “the” three times. Yet all four documents contain it. To compare topics, a term’s repetition within a document and its prevalence across documents answer different questions.

Select a term and a document to inspect their counts. The corpus stays fixed throughout this chapter.

Count documents, not occurrences.

Document frequency, df, counts how many documents contain a term at least once. “Budget” occurs twice in D1, but D1 contributes only one to its document frequency. It appears in two of the four documents.

Term frequency, tf, is the raw count within the selected document in this example.

Rarity becomes an inverse-frequency weight.

We use idf(t) = ln(N / df(t)), where N is the number of documents. A term in every document gets ln(1) = 0. “Budget” gets ln(2) ≈ 0.693; “passed,” found in just one document, gets ln(4) ≈ 1.386.

This is one unsmoothed convention. Alternatives change the formula, term-frequency scaling, and normalization.

Multiply within-document count by rarity.

The TF–IDF weight is tf(t,d) × idf(t). In D1, “budget” receives 2 × 0.693 ≈ 1.386. “The” receives 3 × 0 = 0. The coordinates still name vocabulary terms; only their weights have changed.

This weighting targets distinctiveness in this corpus. Rare terms are not automatically meaningful, and typos can be rare too.

Similarity still depends on shared words.

Cosine similarity compares vector direction, dividing the dot product by both vector lengths. But “senate” and “parliament” remain separate coordinates. TF–IDF alone does not learn that words used in similar contexts may be related.

For predictive evaluation, learn the vocabulary and IDF weights on training documents, then apply them to held-out texts.

word2vec

Can different words share information?

As vocabulary indicators, “senate” and “parliament” point along separate axes: their one-hot vectors have dot product zero. Word2vec instead learns a short, dense vector for each word type from its use in a corpus.

We now represent words, rather than whole documents. The two-dimensional parameters below are constructed for this explanation.

Make a prediction from nearby words.

In continuous bag of words (CBOW), context words predict a center word. A window of radius one around “senate” supplies “the” and “debated.” Sliding the window through texts creates many training examples.

Skip-gram reverses the prediction: a center word predicts nearby words. Both are word2vec training architectures.

Look up vectors and average them.

The model retrieves an input vector for each context word and averages them. Our two context vectors average to [0.50, 0.50]. CBOW ignores order within this local context window.

A second table holds output vectors for candidate target words. The two tables play different roles during training.

Turn compatibility scores into probabilities.

A dot product scores each candidate against the context average. Softmax exponentiates and normalizes the scores so the probabilities sum to one. The loss is −ln P(senate | context): it falls when the target becomes more probable.

This six-word model uses full softmax for transparency. Large-vocabulary training commonly uses hierarchical softmax or negative sampling.

A gradient update changes the vectors.

Scroll to apply one simultaneous gradient step to the input and output vectors. In this example, the target probability rises from 21.0% to 26.4%, and the loss falls from 1.559 to 1.331. Repeated local predictions shape the embedding tables.

The displayed update uses learning rate 0.5. These are exact calculations for illustrative parameters, not a fitted corpus result.

Shared contexts create shared learning pressure.

“The parliament debated” and “the senate debated” give the same context with different targets. Across many examples, shared contexts help words acquire related representations. They need not be synonyms: antonyms can also occur in similar contexts.

Standard word2vec has one vector per word type and can inherit corpus biases. A document vector requires an additional choice, such as averaging its word vectors.
