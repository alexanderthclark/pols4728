Begin with four short documents.

Suppose we want to compare what political texts discuss. These four constructed documents are small enough to inspect word by word. A computer needs a representation that lets the same operation compare every pair.

D1–D4 are teaching examples, not a sample of parliamentary speech.

Tokenize the text.

Tokenization splits a text into units called tokens. For these English examples, each uninterrupted sequence of letters is a token. We also lowercase the text and remove punctuation, so “The” and “the” count as the same term. D1 contains eight tokens, including three occurrences of “the” and two of “budget.” Each occurrence is a token; the shared vocabulary will contain each distinct term once.

Tokens need not be whole words. Character tokens retain individual letters and symbols; subword tokens split words into smaller pieces; a multiword expression such as “New York” can be kept as one unit. Token boundaries, lowercasing, and punctuation handling are separate decisions. Retaining punctuation would be necessary to count the em dashes in our opening example. The research question determines which distinctions should survive.

Group inflections with lemmatization.

Lemmatization maps an inflected word to its dictionary form, or lemma. For example, “debated” and “debating” can be counted under “debate,” and “senators” under “senator.” Pooling these forms can make a representation less sparse, but it also removes distinctions in how the text was written. Choosing a lemma can depend on part of speech and context. Stemming is a different approach that typically uses rules to strip word endings; its output need not be a dictionary word.

A lemma groups inflections, not all related words: “senate” and “parliament” remain different terms. We do not lemmatize or stem our four example documents.

Decide whether to remove stop words.

A stop-word list specifies terms to exclude from a representation. Such lists often contain frequent grammatical words such as “the,” “and,” and “of,” which may contribute little to a topical comparison. In D1, removing the three occurrences of “the” would leave five tokens. This is a choice about the information we want to retain, not a required cleaning step. Common words can help distinguish writing habits, and removing a negation such as “not” can erase a consequential difference in meaning.

There is no universal list that suits every language, corpus, or research question. Our worked matrix retains all the tokenized words, including “the”; the Federalist example will show why common words can be useful.

Give every document the same coordinates.

For our worked example, we lowercase the texts and retain alphabetic tokens, without removing stop words or applying lemmatization. Each column is a vocabulary term; each row is a document. A cell counts how often that term occurs. D1 has two instances of “budget” and three of “the.” Most cells in a realistic document–term matrix are zero.

Scroll back: the words return to their original positions. The count representation has one coordinate for every vocabulary term.

The bag loses word order.

“Senators support voters” and “Voters support senators” produce identical counts. Their meanings differ because the roles reverse. Counting can still be useful, but this representation cannot distinguish these sentences.

Bigrams can retain some local order, at the cost of a larger vocabulary.

Small words can reveal writing habits.

In the classic Federalist Papers study, Mosteller and Wallace (1963) compared common-word frequencies in Hamilton’s and Madison’s writings. Their combined evidence favored Madison for the twelve disputed essays. Also discussed in Grimmer, Roberts, and Stewart (2022).

The chart shows approximate reported rates of “upon” in their comparison writings, including material outside the Federalist Papers. One word did not settle the case.

From writing style to subject matter.

Authorship and topical comparison ask different questions of the same counts. In the Federalist example, common function words carry stylistic information, so removing them automatically could discard the signal. To compare what documents discuss, we may instead emphasize terms that distinguish them from other texts in the corpus. TF–IDF combines repetition within a document with prevalence across documents to assign each term a weight. We will keep the same vocabulary and change the weights, rather than remove terms using a fixed stop-word list.

Compare rates or account for length when texts differ in size. Counting words cannot by itself separate authorship from genre and topic. Feature selection and weighting should follow the research question.

TF–IDF

A frequent word need not be distinctive.

Return to D1: “the” occurs three times, but it also appears in every other document. “Budget” occurs in only two. For topical comparison, repetition within a document and prevalence across documents tell us different things.

The corpus stays fixed throughout this chapter. We first inspect “the” in D1, then follow “budget” through document frequency, inverse document frequency, and TF–IDF weighting.

Count documents, not occurrences.

Document frequency, df⁡(t)\operatorname{df}(t)df(t), counts how many documents contain a term at least once. “Budget” occurs twice in D1, but D1 contributes only one to its document frequency. It appears in two of the four documents.

Term frequency, tf⁡(t,d)\operatorname{tf}(t,d)tf(t,d), is the raw count within document ddd in this example.

Rarity becomes an inverse-frequency weight.

We use idf⁡(t)=ln⁡ ⁣(N/df⁡(t))\operatorname{idf}(t)=\ln\!\left(N/\operatorname{df}(t)\right)idf(t)=ln(N/df(t)), where NNN is the number of documents. A term in every document gets ln⁡(1)=0\ln(1)=0ln(1)=0. “Budget” gets ln⁡(2)≈0.693\ln(2)\approx0.693ln(2)≈0.693; “passed,” found in just one document, gets ln⁡(4)≈1.386\ln(4)\approx1.386ln(4)≈1.386.

This is one unsmoothed convention. Alternatives change the formula, term-frequency scaling, and normalization.

Multiply within-document count by rarity.

The TF–IDF weight is tf⁡(t,d)×idf⁡(t)\operatorname{tf}(t,d)\times\operatorname{idf}(t)tf(t,d)×idf(t). In D1, “budget” receives 2×0.693≈1.3862\times0.693\approx1.3862×0.693≈1.386. “The” receives 3×0=03\times0=03×0=0. The coordinates still name vocabulary terms; only their weights have changed.

This weighting targets distinctiveness in this corpus. Rare terms are not automatically meaningful, and typos can be rare too.

Similarity still depends on shared words.

Cosine similarity compares vector direction, dividing the dot product by both vector lengths. But “senate” and “parliament” remain separate coordinates. TF–IDF alone does not learn that words used in similar contexts may be related.

For predictive evaluation, learn the vocabulary and IDF weights on training documents, then apply them to held-out texts.

Choosing a representation

What should similarity mean?

A representation makes some differences visible and others hard to see. An authorship study, a document search, and a study of political language may need different features—even when they begin with the same texts.
