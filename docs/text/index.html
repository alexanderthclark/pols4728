Turn features into counts.

Bag of words

Count the word frequencies.

Imagine catching each word of a speech in a bag. Our Lincoln-like speaker says “government of the people, by the people, for the people.” Scroll to send each separate word from the speaker to the bag.

The fragment is from the Gettysburg Address. Scroll slowly to follow individual words into the bag.

Keep repetition.

The bag contains ten tokens but only six distinct terms. “The” and “people” each occur three times. Counts retain repetition while discarding where each word appeared.

A bag-of-words vector records one count for each vocabulary term.

But lose word order.

Counts preserve repetition, but lose word order. “Senators support voters” and “Voters support senators” produce identical counts. Their meanings differ because the roles reverse. Counting can still be useful, but this representation cannot distinguish these sentences.

Bigrams can retain some local order, at the cost of a larger vocabulary.

Different words, similar ideas.

The reverse problem also occurs: different words can express similar ideas. Compare three short sentences: 1. “Plebiscites are unwise.” 2. “Democracy is foolish.” 3. “Majority rule rules.” The first two criticize forms of democratic decision-making; the third praises majority rule. Can word counts capture that resemblance?

The first two express a similar negative stance, though they are not exact synonyms.

Count these words, too.

Use the vocabulary plebiscites, are, unwise, democracy, is, foolish, majority, rule, rules, in that order. Each sentence contains three words, each occurring once. Word overlap misses the shared negative stance of sentences 1 and 2. Different wording can obscure similar meanings, just as identical counts can obscure different meanings in the senators/voters example.

Now compare two longer passages.

Now apply the same representation to two longer passages. Our first document is Joseph Schumpeter’s sentence: “Thus the typical citizen drops down to a lower level of mental performance as soon as he enters the political field.” Our second is Walt Whitman’s: “Political Democracy, as it exists and practically works in America, with all its threatening evils, supplies a training school for making grand young men.” Their contrasting accounts of political life give us two documents to represent and compare.

The quotations are from Schumpeter’s Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy and Whitman’s Democratic Vistas. Each quoted passage is one document; the corpus contains these two passages. The research question guides which word forms we combine, which words we retain, and how we weight them. We use these same two documents throughout preprocessing and TF–IDF.

Choose the units to count.

Tokenization splits a text into units called tokens. For these English examples, each uninterrupted sequence of letters is a token. We also lowercase the text and remove punctuation, so “Political” and “political” count as the same term. Schumpeter’s quotation contains 21 tokens: “the” and “as” each occur twice. Whitman’s contains 24 tokens. Each occurrence is a token; the shared vocabulary will contain each distinct term once.

Tokens need not be whole words. Character tokens retain individual letters and symbols; subword tokens split words into smaller pieces; a multiword expression such as “New York” can be kept as one unit. Token boundaries, lowercasing, and punctuation handling are separate decisions. Retaining punctuation would be necessary to count the em dashes in our opening example. The research question determines which distinctions should survive.

Group inflected forms.

Lemmatization maps an inflected word to its dictionary form, or lemma. In these quotations, “drops” becomes “drop,” “enters” becomes “enter,” and “men” becomes “man.” We also use “exist,” “work,” “threaten,” “evil,” “supply,” and “make” for Whitman’s inflected forms. Pooling such forms can make a representation less sparse, but it also removes distinctions in how the text was written. Choosing a lemma can depend on part of speech and context. Stemming is a different approach that typically uses rules to strip word endings; its output need not be a dictionary word.

A lemma groups inflections, not all related words: “citizen” and “democracy” remain different terms. Lemmatization is optional; here we apply it before building the count matrix.

Choose which words to keep.

A stop-word list specifies terms to exclude from a representation. Such lists often contain frequent grammatical words such as “the,” “and,” and “of,” which may contribute little to a topical comparison. After lemmatization and the selected stop-word removal, Schumpeter’s quotation becomes “typical citizen drop down lower level mental performance enter political field”: 11 tokens, after removing 10 of the original 21. Whitman’s becomes “political democracy exist practically work america threaten evil supply training school make grand young man”: 15 tokens, after removing 9 of the original 24. These are the processed documents used throughout the count and TF–IDF examples.

This is a choice about the information we want to retain, not a required cleaning step. There is no universal list that suits every language, corpus, or research question. Common words can help distinguish writing habits, and removing a negation such as “not” can erase a consequential difference in meaning. The authorship application at the end illustrates why common words can be useful.

TF–IDF

Some words distinguish documents better.

The count matrix gives every occurrence the same contribution. Some words, however, distinguish these documents better than others. “Political” occurs once in each quotation. “Citizen” occurs once in Schumpeter and is absent from Whitman. Their counts within Schumpeter match, but their prevalence across the corpus differs.

The corpus stays fixed throughout the document-level TF–IDF walkthrough. We first inspect “political” in Schumpeter’s quotation, then follow “citizen” through document frequency, inverse document frequency, and TF–IDF weighting.

Count how many documents.

Document frequency, dft\mathrm{df}_tdft​, counts how many documents contain a term at least once. “Citizen” occurs in Schumpeter’s quotation and is absent from Whitman’s, so its document frequency is 1. “Political” appears in both, so its document frequency is 2. Repeating a word within a document would increase its term frequency but would not increase its document frequency.

Term frequency is the raw count ct,dc_{t,d}ct,d​ of term ttt within document ddd in this example.

Downweight shared words.

We use idft=ln⁡ ⁣(N/dft)\mathrm{idf}_t=\ln\!\left(N/\mathrm{df}_t\right)idft​=ln(N/dft​), where N=2N=2N=2 is the number of documents and ln⁡\lnln denotes the natural logarithm. “Political” appears in both and gets ln⁡(2/2)=0\ln(2/2)=0ln(2/2)=0. Every other retained term appears in only one quotation and gets ln⁡(2/1)=ln⁡(2)≈0.693\ln(2/1)=\ln(2)\approx0.693ln(2/1)=ln(2)≈0.693. A zero weight here means that the term does not distinguish these two documents; it does not mean that the word is unimportant to their arguments.

This is one unsmoothed convention. Alternatives change the formula, term-frequency scaling, and normalization.

Combine count and rarity.

TF–IDF assigns a weight to a word–document pair, relative to a fixed corpus: wt,d=ct,dln⁡ ⁣(N/dft)w_{t,d}=c_{t,d}\ln\!\left(N/\mathrm{df}_t\right)wt,d​=ct,d​ln(N/dft​). Here ct,dc_{t,d}ct,d​ is the raw count of word ttt in document ddd, NNN is the number of documents, and dft\mathrm{df}_tdft​ counts documents containing that word. The IDF factor is shared across documents, while the count can differ. “Citizen” appears in one of the two documents, so its IDF is ln⁡(2/1)\ln(2/1)ln(2/1). Its score in Schumpeter is 1ln⁡(2/1)≈0.6931\ln(2/1)\approx0.6931ln(2/1)≈0.693; in Whitman, the count and score are both zero.

These are raw-count, unsmoothed weights. Other TF–IDF conventions rescale counts, smooth IDF, or normalize document vectors.

Apply this to every cell.

The TF–IDF weight is wt,d=ct,d idftw_{t,d}=c_{t,d}\,\mathrm{idf}_twt,d​=ct,d​idft​. In Schumpeter’s quotation, “citizen” receives 1×ln⁡(2)≈0.6931\times\ln(2)\approx0.6931×ln(2)≈0.693. In Whitman’s quotation, “america” receives the same weight. “Political” receives 1×0=01\times0=01×0=0 in both. Each remaining term receives ln⁡(2)\ln(2)ln(2) in the document containing it and zero in the other. These weights are shown before length normalization.

This weighting targets distinctiveness in this corpus. Rare terms are not automatically meaningful, and typos can be rare too.

Compare the weighted vectors.

Apply the same cosine comparison to the complete 25-term TF–IDF vectors. Both document vectors are nonzero, but their only shared term, “political,” receives zero weight. Every other term appears in just one document, so the dot product and cosine similarity are zero. As with the three short sentences, zero word overlap does not imply unrelated meanings: both quotations concern political life. TF–IDF changes the weights, but still compares vocabulary coordinates.

TF–IDF does not learn semantic relationships among different words. Embeddings can group semantically related texts, and TF–IDF can then help describe those groups. For predictive evaluation, learn the vocabulary and IDF weights on training documents, then apply them to held-out texts.

Application: Authorship

Common words reveal writing habits.

The usefulness of a word depends on the research question. Authorship and topical comparison ask different questions of word counts: common function words can carry stylistic information, so removing them automatically could discard the signal. In the classic Federalist Papers study, Mosteller and Wallace (1963) compared common-word frequencies in Hamilton’s and Madison’s writings. Their combined evidence favored Madison for the twelve disputed essays. Also discussed in Grimmer, Roberts, and Stewart (2022).

The chart shows approximate reported rates of “upon” in their comparison writings, including material outside the Federalist Papers. One word did not settle the case. Compare rates or account for length when texts differ in size. Counting words cannot by itself separate authorship from genre and topic. Feature selection and weighting should follow the research question.

Application: BERTopic

Use word counts to describe groups.

BERTopic first groups document embeddings into clusters. It then pools each cluster’s documents into one bag of words. We now weight a word within a whole cluster, rather than within an individual document.

We now use a separate synthetic corpus with four short texts in two supplied groups. These texts are independent of the Schumpeter and Whitman quotations. The standard BERTopic workflow reduces embedding dimensions before clustering; its subsequent bag-of-words representation describes the resulting groups. The illustration supplies the groups to isolate that second task.

Then weight the pooled words.

BERTopic’s class-based TF–IDF assigns a weight to a word–cluster pair. Its default formula is cTFIDF⁡(t,c)=f(t,c)Lcln⁡ ⁣(1+Af(t,all))\operatorname{cTFIDF}(t,c)=\frac{f(t,c)}{L_c}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{A}{f(t,\mathrm{all})}\right)cTFIDF(t,c)=Lc​f(t,c)​ln(1+f(t,all)A​). Here f(t,c)f(t,c)f(t,c) counts the word’s occurrences in the pooled cluster, LcL_cLc​ is that cluster’s total token count, AAA is the average pooled cluster length (truncated to an integer in the implementation), and f(t,all)f(t,\mathrm{all})f(t,all) counts the word’s occurrences across all clusters. This denominator counts occurrences, not documents or clusters containing the word. In this constructed example, “budget” occurs twice among cluster A’s six tokens, and twice across both clusters. With average cluster length six, its weight is 26ln⁡(1+6/2)≈0.462\frac{2}{6}\ln(1+6/2)\approx0.46262​ln(1+6/2)≈0.462. This describes “budget” in cluster A; it is not a score for any one original document. BERTopic uses the highest-weighted words to describe each topic.

Application: LDA and STM

Probabilities live on a simplex.

A probability vector for KKK categories belongs to the (K−1)(K-1)(K−1)-dimensional simplex. With three categories, the simplex is a triangle. Its vertices put all probability on one category; points along an edge mix two categories; interior points give positive probability to all three. The illustrated vector θ=(0.5,0.2,0.3)\boldsymbol\theta=(0.5,0.2,0.3)θ=(0.5,0.2,0.3) could parameterize a categorical draw over Movement, Breath, and Reflection. The triangle contains the possible vectors; a probability distribution over the triangle tells us which vectors are more plausible.

These three topics and all numerical values in the diagrams are constructed teaching examples. In an actual fit, researchers choose the number of topics and interpret their learned word distributions and representative documents before naming them.

A Dirichlet draws a probability vector.

The Dirichlet is a distribution over probability vectors, so its draws automatically satisfy the nonnegativity and sum-to-one constraints. Its density is proportional to ∏kθkαk−1\prod_k\theta_k^{\alpha_k-1}∏k​θkαk​−1​. Once a vector is drawn, a categorical draw selects one of its categories using those probabilities. The Dirichlet is also conjugate to categorical sampling: if the category assignments are observed with counts n=(n1,…,nK)\mathbf n=(n_1,\ldots,n_K)n=(n1​,…,nK​), then the posterior is Dirichlet⁡(α+n)\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol\alpha+\mathbf n)Dirichlet(α+n). This simple update is another reason it is useful.

This conjugate update assumes known category assignments. In LDA, the token-level topic assignments are latent, so their counts must be inferred.

Concentration changes the mixtures.

Compare Dirichlet⁡(0.3,0.3,0.3)\operatorname{Dirichlet}(0.3,0.3,0.3)Dirichlet(0.3,0.3,0.3), Dirichlet⁡(1,1,1)\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,1,1)Dirichlet(1,1,1), and Dirichlet⁡(8,8,8)\operatorname{Dirichlet}(8,8,8)Dirichlet(8,8,8). The first often produces mixtures dominated by one topic; the second is uniform over the simplex; the third concentrates near equal shares. Their common mean is (1/3,1/3,1/3)(1/3,1/3,1/3)(1/3,1/3,1/3). More generally, the relative sizes of the parameters determine the mean, while their sum α0\alpha_0α0​ determines concentration around it. Scaling every parameter by the same positive factor changes concentration while preserving the mean.

Shading is rescaled within each plot to show relative density, so compare where it concentrates rather than color intensity across plots. Uniform density over the triangle does not mean every drawn vector has equal components. A single draw can be far from the center even when the mean is at the center.

Each vector has VVV components and lives on a vocabulary simplex. The table shows three selected words plus their remaining probability mass. Its Movement row is (0.55,0.10,0.02,0.33)(0.55,0.10,0.02,0.33)(0.55,0.10,0.02,0.33), Breath row is (0.05,0.60,0.01,0.34)(0.05,0.60,0.01,0.34)(0.05,0.60,0.01,0.34), and Reflection row is (0.05,0.05,0.20,0.70)(0.05,0.05,0.20,0.70)(0.05,0.05,0.20,0.70), in the order cradle, inhale, precious, and all other words.

Each class gets its own topic mixture.

Latent Dirichlet allocation models a corpus with shared topics and document-specific topic mixtures. For each token, first choose a topic.

The assignment zdnz_{dn}zdn​ is a latent category in {1,…,K}\{1,\ldots,K\}{1,…,K}. With θd=(0.5,0.2,0.3)\boldsymbol\theta_d=(0.5,0.2,0.3)θd​=(0.5,0.2,0.3), a categorical draw selects Movement with probability 0.5, Breath with probability 0.2, or Reflection with probability 0.3. Topic names are interpretations of learned distributions. A model can find patterns that cut across the substantive categories a researcher initially expected.

STM lets metadata shape topic prevalence.

A structural topic model incorporates document covariates during estimation. Its logistic-normal prevalence model uses K−1K-1K−1 latent log-ratios ηd\boldsymbol\eta_dηd​, with a baseline topic fixed at zero. Their normal mean is Γ⊤xd\boldsymbol\Gamma^\top\mathbf x_dΓ⊤xd​, where xd\mathbf x_dxd​ may include instructor group and class type. Their covariance Σ\boldsymbol\SigmaΣ allows dependence between log-ratios. The transformation θdk=eηdk/(1+∑j<Keηdj)\theta_{dk}=e^{\eta_{dk}}/(1+\sum_{j<K}e^{\eta_{dj}})θdk​=eηdk​/(1+∑j<K​eηdj​) for k<Kk<Kk<K, with θdK=1/(1+∑j<Keηdj)\theta_{dK}=1/(1+\sum_{j<K}e^{\eta_{dj}})θdK​=1/(1+∑j<K​eηdj​), produces a valid simplex vector.

Metadata shifts the distribution of mixtures; it does not assign a known topic to each class. The model estimates its metadata relationships together with the topics. In general, softmax⁡(E[ηd],0)≠E[θd]\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\eta_d],0)\neq\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\theta_d]softmax(E[ηd​],0)=E[θd​]. Content covariates change words within a topic.

A content covariate ydy_dyd​ allows the word distribution for topic kkk to depend on a document’s group. For example, a movement topic might emphasize different words in yoga and cycling classes. STM can construct vocabulary logits from a shared word baseline, topic deviations, covariate-group deviations, and topic-by-group interactions, then normalize them with a softmax over words. This changes βk(yd)\boldsymbol\beta_k(y_d)βk​(yd​), even if the document’s topic mixture is held fixed. It is separate from a prevalence covariate’s influence on θd\boldsymbol\theta_dθd​.

The course’s existing Peloton STM uses instructor group and class type for prevalence, with no content covariate. The final diagram illustrates the optional content mechanism rather than a result from that fit. Both LDA and STM retain a bag-of-words likelihood; neither learns word order from these counts.

Choosing a representation

What should similarity mean?

A representation makes some differences visible and others hard to see. An authorship study, a document search, and a study of political language may need different features—even when they begin with the same texts.
