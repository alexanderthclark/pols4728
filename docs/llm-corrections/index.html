1 / Correcting LLM measurements

Correcting LLM measurements

An LLM can assign a score to every document. We want estimates of the quantity an expert would have coded. A random expert audit lets us estimate the labeling errors and correct the downstream analysis.

Full-corpus prediction+audit correction\text{Full-corpus prediction} + \text{audit correction}Full-corpus prediction+audit correction

We hold predictions fixed before drawing a simple random expert audit.

2 / Three procedures

Validate, then use the labels

The familiar workflow predicts labels, checks them against an expert-coded subset, and revises the predictions if needed. It then uses the predicted labels directly in an average or regression. The accuracy check does not change that final estimator.

Qi⟶average or regressionQ_i \longrightarrow \text{average or regression}Qi​⟶average or regression

An audit used only for validation does not correct the downstream estimate.

3 / Three procedures

Train, then use the predictions

Another workflow begins with expert labels, trains a prediction model, and uses cross-validation to assess and tune it. Its predictions then become variables in the analysis. Better prediction can help, but remaining errors still enter the downstream estimator.

X^i⟶average or regression\widehat X_i \longrightarrow \text{average or regression}Xi​⟶average or regression

Cross-validation evaluates predictions; it does not itself correct the statistical analysis.

4 / Three procedures

Use the audit in the estimator

DSL combines predictions for all documents with expert labels from a probability sample. The paper permits an additional prediction-improvement step. We skip it here and use the original LLM scores, followed by a correction to the quantities the analysis needs.

Predicted moments+audited discrepancies\text{Predicted moments} + \text{audited discrepancies}Predicted moments+audited discrepancies

Known sampling probabilities supply the design; no estimated sampling model is needed here.

5 / Correcting an average

What share expresses support?

Let X be the expert-coded support indicator and Q the automated indicator. The LLM labels 4,000 of 10,000 statements as supportive. We know its average is 40%; the unknown quantity is the average experts would report.

Q‾A=4,00010,000=0.40\overline Q_A=\frac{4{,}000}{10{,}000}=0.40Q​A​=10,0004,000​=0.40

A denotes all documents. This binary illustration is separate from the regression example.

6 / Correcting an average

Compare paired labels

On the same audited statements, experts report 30% support and the LLM reports 36%. There are 90 false positives and 30 false negatives. Their signed difference estimates how much the automated average is overstated.

(X−Q)‾S=30−901,000=−0.06\overline{(X-Q)}_S=\frac{30-90}{1{,}000}=-0.06(X−Q)​S​=1,00030−90​=−0.06

S denotes the random audit. Compare expert and automated labels on the same documents.

7 / Correcting an average

Add the audit correction

Start with the automated average over all 10,000 statements. Add the average expert-minus-automated discrepancy from the random audit. The estimated expert-coded share becomes 34%. We use the full corpus for the prediction and the audit for its error.

μ^=Q‾A+(X−Q)‾S=0.40+(0.30−0.36)=0.34\widehat\mu=\overline Q_A+\overline{(X-Q)}_S=0.40+(0.30-0.36)=0.34μ​=Q​A​+(X−Q)​S​=0.40+(0.30−0.36)=0.34

For a broader population interpretation, the corpus must represent that population.

8 / Correcting an average

Why does the audit work?

For every document, the expert score equals its prediction plus the expert-minus-prediction discrepancy. Random sampling makes the audit’s average discrepancy unbiased for the corpus’s average discrepancy. This argument works for continuous scores as well as binary labels.

X‾A=Q‾A+(X−Q)‾A\overline X_A=\overline Q_A+\overline{(X-Q)}_AXA​=Q​A​+(X−Q)​A​

The audit estimates the last term. It does not require errors to be unrelated to other variables.

9 / Using a score as a feature

Regress engagement on hostility

Now X is expert-coded hostility and Y is engagement. The automated score Q overstates hostility for emphatic writing and understates it for restrained writing. Style also predicts engagement. Regressing engagement on the automated score gives a slope of 3.111.

Q=X+0.5Z,Y=20+2X+6Z+εQ=X+0.5Z,\qquad Y=20+2X+6Z+\varepsilonQ=X+0.5Z,Y=20+2X+6Z+ε

Z is writing style. The target is the descriptive regression of Y on X, with an intercept.

10 / Using a score as a feature

The mean is right; the slope is wrong

Move each point from its automated score to its expert score. The average stays at 3, but the fitted slope falls to 2.000. The labeling errors cancel in the mean while remaining associated with engagement. Measurement error need not attenuate a slope.

Q‾A=X‾A=3,βexpert=2\overline Q_A=\overline X_A=3,\qquad \beta_{\mathrm{expert}}=2Q​A​=XA​=3,βexpert​=2

All expert labels are visible only to explain this synthetic benchmark; the estimator will not use them.

11 / Using a score as a feature

What can we actually observe?

Return to the information available to the analyst: automated scores and engagement for every post, and expert scores for a simple random audit of 16 posts. Each correction will use only these paired expert and automated measurements.

Pr⁡(i∈S)=1640=0.40\Pr(i\in S)=\frac{16}{40}=0.40Pr(i∈S)=4016​=0.40

The automated scores were fixed before this audit; no training or tuning uses the audited labels.

12 / Using a score as a feature

Shifting the feature leaves its slope

Suppose we added the same constant to every automated score. The point cloud and fitted line would shift horizontally, leaving the slope unchanged. Correcting a feature’s mean cannot repair the covariance and variance that determine its regression coefficient.

Cov⁡(Q+c,Y)Var⁡(Q+c)=Cov⁡(Q,Y)Var⁡(Q)\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Q+c,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(Q+c)}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Q,Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(Q)}Var(Q+c)Cov(Q+c,Y)​=Var(Q)Cov(Q,Y)​

The illustration shifts scores by 0.5. The actual audit’s mean correction in this example is zero.

13 / Correcting the regression moments

Correct the first moment

The regression needs the mean of expert hostility. Apply the same correction as before: the full-corpus automated mean plus the audit’s average labeling discrepancy. In this particular audit, positive and negative discrepancies cancel, so the corrected mean remains 3.

MX=Q‾A+(X−Q)‾S=3+0=3M_X=\overline Q_A+\overline{(X-Q)}_S=3+0=3MX​=Q​A​+(X−Q)​S​=3+0=3

M denotes an estimated expert-coded moment. Zero average error does not finish the correction.

14 / Correcting the regression moments

Correct the second moment

The denominator also needs the mean squared expert score. Compare squared expert and squared automated scores within the audit. Add their average difference to the full-corpus automated second moment. Squaring a mean-corrected feature would perform a different calculation.

MX2=Q2‾A+(X2−Q2)‾SM_{X^2}=\overline{Q^2}_A+\overline{(X^2-Q^2)}_SMX2​=Q2​A​+(X2−Q2)​S​

Here: 11.25 − 0.1875 = 11.0625. The mean squared score differs from the squared mean score.

15 / Correcting the regression moments

Correct the cross-moment

The numerator needs the expert score multiplied by engagement. On the audit, multiply each labeling discrepancy by that post’s engagement. This correction detects the association between labeling error and the downstream outcome, even when the average labeling error is zero.

MXY=QY‾A+(X−Q)Y‾S=85−2.75=82.25M_{XY}=\overline{QY}_A+\overline{(X-Q)Y}_S=85-2.75=82.25MXY​=QY​A​+(X−Q)Y​S​=85−2.75=82.25

Engagement is observed for every post; only expert hostility requires the audit.

16 / Correcting the regression moments

Assemble the corrected slope

Use the corrected moments in the usual slope formula. Engagement is fully observed, so its mean uses all 40 posts. The corrected covariance is 4.25 and the corrected variance is 2.0625. Their ratio gives the DSL estimate.

β^DSL=MXY−MXY‾AMX2−MX2=4.252.0625≈2.061\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{DSL}}=\frac{M_{XY}-M_X\overline Y_A}{M_{X^2}-M_X^2}=\frac{4.25}{2.0625}\approx2.061β​DSL​=MX2​−MX2​MXY​−MX​YA​​=2.06254.25​≈2.061

The moments are design-unbiased; their nonlinear ratio need not be exactly unbiased in a small sample.

17 / What the correction achieves

Compare the four estimates

The expert-only regression uses the audited posts and gives 1.706. DSL uses that same expert-label budget, plus automated measurements from the whole corpus, and gives 2.061. The full-expert benchmark is 2.000; the automated-feature regression remains at 3.111.

β^DSL≈2.061,βbenchmark=2.000\widehat\beta_{\mathrm{DSL}}\approx2.061,\qquad\beta_{\mathrm{benchmark}}=2.000β​DSL​≈2.061,βbenchmark​=2.000

These are results for one random audit, not a guarantee about every sample.

18 / What the correction achieves

The correction also needs a standard error

Imagine repeatedly sampling a new corpus of posts and drawing a new expert audit. For this population interpretation, the linearized standard error is about 1.16 for expert-only regression and 0.80 for DSL, using the same 16 expert labels.

SE^expert only≈1.16,SE^DSL≈0.80\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{expert\ only}}\approx1.16,\qquad\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\mathrm{DSL}}\approx0.80SEexpert only​≈1.16,SEDSL​≈0.80

These SEs are first-order approximations. Fixed-corpus audit RMSEs, 0.979 and 0.194, describe a different experiment.

19 / The general pattern

Correct the quantity your analysis uses

For an average, correct the predicted average. For a score used as a regression feature, correct the moments entering that regression. Full-corpus predictions supply information; paired expert measurements estimate their discrepancies. The audit’s sampling design makes those corrections interpretable.

Mg=g(Q,Y)‾A+g(X,Y)−g(Q,Y)‾SM_g=\overline{g(Q,Y)}_A+\overline{g(X,Y)-g(Q,Y)}_SMg​=g(Q,Y)​A​+g(X,Y)−g(Q,Y)​S​

Precision gains depend on prediction quality. Learning predictions from the audit requires additional treatment.
