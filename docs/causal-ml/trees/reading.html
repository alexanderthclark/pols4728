Causal trees

Watch a covariate space become a tree, compare outcome and effect splits, and separate subgroup discovery from estimation.

The main arithmetic example has 64 constructed observations, two covariates, equal treatment and control counts, and treatment effects of 6 or 2. A second fixed realization supplies honest estimates of 6.4 and 1.8. A clearly separate constant-effect example demonstrates selection on noise. These are chosen realizations of a hypothetical randomized experiment, not empirical findings, fitted software output, or a coverage simulation.

An average can hide heterogeneity.

The overall treatment–control difference is 4 points. A single number gives every respondent the same prediction of their group’s effect.

τ^root=Y‾1−Y‾0=27−23=4\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{root}}=\overline Y_1-\overline Y_0=27-23=4τroot​=Y1​−Y0​=27−23=4

In this constructed population, the treatment effect is 6 for lower-engagement respondents and 2 for higher-engagement respondents. Equal group shares give an ATE of 4. We know those potential-outcome differences because we created the example. A researcher observes only one outcome per respondent and must learn effect heterogeneity from treatment–control comparisons.

3 / 16 · From outcomes to effects

The best outcome split can miss effect differences.

Split on baseline knowledge. Average outcomes change from 22 to 28, but both leaves have the same treatment effect of 4.

τ^X2<0.5=τ^X2≥0.5=4\widehat\tau_{X_2<0.5}=\widehat\tau_{X_2\ge0.5}=4τX2​<0.5​=τX2​≥0.5​=4

An ordinary outcome tree rewards reductions in squared error for YYY. In this balanced example, splitting X2X_2X2​ at 0.5 reduces mean squared error by 9, while splitting X1X_1X1​ at 0.5 reduces it by only 1. The first split captures a strong baseline-outcome difference. It does not, by itself, separate treatment effects. Outcome prediction and effect prediction therefore favor different initial splits.

Compare treatment contrasts within each leaf.

Split on engagement instead. The left leaf has a 6-point effect; the right leaf has a 2-point effect.

τ^L=29−23=6,τ^R=25−23=2\widehat\tau_L=29-23=6,\qquad \widehat\tau_R=25-23=2τL​=29−23=6,τR​=25−23=2

The partition turns covariate values into subgroup membership. Within each proposed leaf, compare the treated and control mean outcomes. Randomization at a constant probability makes these comparisons identify leaf average effects in the population. The resulting predictions are group averages, not recovered individual treatment effects.

7 / 16 · Choosing splits

A second split can improve precision.

Now split each engagement group on baseline knowledge. Effects stay at 6 and 2, but outcomes within leaves become less variable.

Q(Πfour)=20−0.71=19.29Q(\Pi_{\mathrm{four}})=20-0.71=19.29Q(Πfour​)=20−0.71=19.29

A causal tree can use a predictor of outcome levels even when it does not modify treatment effects. Here splitting on baseline knowledge removes within-leaf baseline variation, reducing the penalty from 2.73 to 0.71. The four-leaf score improves to 19.29. Thus the honest objective rewards both useful effect separation and more precise estimation; it does not only search for effect modifiers.

8 / 16 · Choosing splits

Every leaf needs both treatment states.

Another engagement split would leave only four treated and four control observations in each new leaf. The illustrative minimum of eight per arm rejects it.

n1,L≥8,n0,L≥8n_{1,L}\ge8,\qquad n_{0,L}\ge8n1,L​≥8,n0,L​≥8

Minimum treated and control counts prevent unsupported or extremely noisy leaf comparisons. Eight per arm is a demonstration constraint, not a recommendation or a guarantee of reliable normal inference. In actual work, complexity selection and pruning use the structure sample, with a treatment-effect criterion and validation that respect honest estimation. The separate estimation outcomes must not be used to decide which tree to keep.

9 / 16 · Adaptive search

Searching can find a difference in pure noise.

Switch to a population whose true effect is 4 everywhere. In one chosen noisy realization, an engagement split appears to give effects of 6 and 2.

τ(x)=4for every x\tau(x)=4\quad\text{for every }xτ(x)=4for every x

This separate example holds every individual treatment effect at 4. The structure realization happens to give treated observations higher baseline shocks at low engagement and lower shocks at high engagement. Comparing three engagement thresholds favors 0.5 on the unpenalized effect-fit term. The selected contrast is therefore partly a consequence of the search. The values are a deliberately chosen realization, not a claim about the typical size of selection bias.

10 / 16 · Adaptive search

The selected gap need not repeat.

Keep the split fixed and use an independent realization. In this constructed second sample, both leaf estimates return to 4.

τ^L est=τ^R est=4\widehat\tau_L^{\,\mathrm{est}}=\widehat\tau_R^{\,\mathrm{est}}=4τLest​=τRest​=4

The independent estimation outcomes did not help select the partition. Their errors are therefore not selected for making this split look impressive. Here they happen to average to zero in both leaves. Honest estimates will not generally equal the truth in finite samples, and a new sample does not guarantee that a selected difference disappears. The lesson is separation of selection from estimation, not automatic shrinkage to a common effect.

The boundary stays fixed while the sample changes.

11 / 16 · Honest estimation

Separate the jobs before searching.

Use one sample to choose and prune the tree. Reserve independent observations for estimating the effects in its final leaves.

Sstructure∩Sestimation=∅S_{\mathrm{structure}}\cap S_{\mathrm{estimation}}=\varnothingSstructure​∩Sestimation​=∅

Return to the main heterogeneous-effect example. Honest estimation commits to the partition without using the estimation outcomes. All split selection, tuning, and pruning belong to the structure side. The reserved sample then estimates the final leaf contrasts. Honesty sacrifices observations for each task in exchange for protecting leaf estimation from outcome-driven subgroup selection.

Independence of the two samples matters, not merely different labels.

15 / 16 · What a tree estimates

A leaf predicts an average over a region.

For a smoothly changing effect, one leaf still gives one constant prediction. The tree approximates heterogeneity by grouping nearby covariate values.

τL=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X∈L]\tau_L=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X\in L]τL​=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X∈L]

This final target illustration uses a separate smooth effect, τ(x)=6−4x\tau(x)=6-4xτ(x)=6−4x, with XXX uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1]. A split at 0.5 has leaf means 5 and 3. Those averages differ from the effect at many individual covariate values. In the main experiment, randomization identifies leaf contrasts. With observational treatment, merely forming leaves does not remove confounding: conditional exchangeability, overlap, and suitable adjustment are additionally needed.

16 / 16 · What a tree estimates

Honesty and cross-fitting separate different jobs.

Honesty separates partition selection from leaf estimation. Cross-fitting separates nuisance training from evaluating a score on held-out observations.

partition⟶leaf estimatesnuisances⟶held-out score\text{partition}\longrightarrow\text{leaf estimates}\qquad\text{nuisances}\longrightarrow\text{held-out score}partition⟶leaf estimatesnuisances⟶held-out score

The randomized causal tree here needs no nuisance learner to justify within-leaf differences. It is therefore not automatically a DML estimator. Other heterogeneous-effect methods can combine honest partitions with orthogonal scores and cross-fitted nuisance functions, but the roles remain distinct. A single tree is interpretable and can be unstable under changes in the structure sample; forests later aggregate many trees to form adaptive neighborhoods.

Both protect against reuse of outcomes, for different estimation tasks.
