DML for instrumental variables

Build an orthogonal IV score, separate nuisance learning from identification, and see what happens when an instrument is weak or invalid.

The main example is a 24-point factorial construction with coefficient 2 and known conditional means. Every combination of three covariate values and two values each of instrument variation, an unobserved cause, and outcome noise appears once. The weak-instrument scene is a moment-sensitivity calculation. A separate ten-type population illustrates binary IV and LATE. Cross-fitting shows data flow, not browser-trained models.

The treatment still shares an unobserved cause.

3 / 17 · Identification

Bring in an instrument.

An encouragement changes information exposure. Assume it has no direct effect on knowledge and is independent of the structural error given observed covariates.

E[U∣X,Z]=0,E[Z~D~]≠0\mathbb E[U\mid X,Z]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\widetilde Z\widetilde D]\ne0E[U∣X,Z]=0,E[ZD]=0

An encouragement ZZZ is a candidate instrument, not automatically a valid one. The maintained partially linear model excludes a direct effect of ZZZ on YYY, and imposes conditional mean independence of UUU from ZZZ given XXX. Relevance requires a nonzero residual covariance between the instrument and treatment. Well-defined interventions, consistency, and no interference are also part of the causal interpretation. Whether encouragement itself informs respondents is a substantive exclusion question.

Instrument exogeneity, exclusion, and relevance are separate requirements.

6 / 17 · Build the IV score

Measure the first stage.

Moving the instrument residual from −1 to +1 moves mean treatment from −1 to +1. The first-stage slope is 1.

E[Z~D~]E[Z~2]=1\frac{\mathbb E[\widetilde Z\widetilde D]}{\mathbb E[\widetilde Z^2]}=1E[Z2]E[ZD]​=1

The instrument isolates a particular source of treatment variation. Within either value of VVV, the unobserved cause produces dispersion in treatment. Averaging over that cause leaves E[D~∣V]=V\mathbb E[\widetilde D\mid V]=VE[D∣V]=V. The dashed line connects these conditional means; it is not a regression of the outcome on the full treatment residual.

7 / 17 · Build the IV score

Measure the reduced form.

The same instrument change moves mean outcome from −2 to +2. The reduced-form slope is 2.

E[Z~Y~]E[Z~2]=2\frac{\mathbb E[\widetilde Z\widetilde Y]}{\mathbb E[\widetilde Z^2]}=2E[Z2]E[ZY]​=2

The outcome still varies with unobserved motivation. Under the exclusion and exogeneity assumptions, however, the instrument-induced change in its conditional mean operates through treatment. Here E[Y~∣V]=2V\mathbb E[\widetilde Y\mid V]=2VE[Y∣V]=2V, so dividing the reduced-form slope by the first-stage slope recovers the structural coefficient.

The two slopes use the same instrument variation.

8 / 17 · Build the IV score

Take the ratio of pooled moments.

Instrument–outcome covariance is 2. Instrument–treatment covariance is 1. Their ratio recovers the effect of 2.

θ^=∑iZ~iY~i∑iZ~iD~i\widehat\theta=\frac{\sum_i\widetilde Z_i\widetilde Y_i}{\sum_i\widetilde Z_i\widetilde D_i}θ=∑i​Zi​Di​∑i​Zi​Yi​​

With one scalar instrument, the partialling-out estimate is a ratio of these sample cross-products. The 24-point construction gives sums 48 and 24. After cross-fitting, pool the observation-level products before taking the ratio; an unweighted average of fold-specific ratios is generally different. This estimator uses the instrument residual in the moment, rather than the treatment residual used by partially linear regression.

The score balances at the effect.

The score weights the outcome-equation residual by instrument variation unexplained by XXX. At the truth, its population mean is zero because the valid instrument is orthogonal to the structural error. In this construction the average score is 2−θ2-\theta2−θ, which makes the identifying role of the first-stage covariance visible: it determines the slope of the moment equation.

10 / 17 · Orthogonal estimation

Small nuisance errors enter at second order.

Perturb all three conditional means by the same small function. Near the truth, the effect estimate has a flat tangent.

ddtE[ψ(W;θ0,η0+th)]∣t=0=0\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\psi(W;\theta_0,\eta_0+th)]\right|_{t=0}=0dtd​E[ψ(W;θ0​,η0​+th)]​t=0​=0

Here η=(ℓ,m,q)\eta=(\ell,m,q)η=(ℓ,m,q). Add tXtXtX to each true nuisance function. Exact factorial balance gives θ^(t)=(2+23t2)/(1+23t2)\widehat\theta(t)=(2+\frac23t^2)/(1+\frac23t^2)θ(t)=(2+32​t2)/(1+32​t2); the first derivative at zero vanishes. Orthogonality is local protection from nuisance error, not protection from a direct instrument effect or unobserved instrument confounding.

11 / 17 · Orthogonal estimation

Fit elsewhere. Predict here.

Train three nuisance learners outside each held-out fold. Rotate the held-out fold, then assemble every observation’s residual products.

η^i=η^(−k)(Xi),i∈Ik\widehat\eta_i=\widehat\eta^{(-k)}(X_i),\qquad i\in I_kη​i​=η​(−k)(Xi​),i∈Ik​

The held-out observation must not help train its own nuisance predictions. Model selection and tuning also stay inside the corresponding training data. Once every observation has held-out predictions, solve the pooled IV moment. Cross-fitting reduces overfitting dependence; it does not itself imply accurate nuisance functions or validate an instrument. The schematic rotates four folds without fitting any models.

12 / 17 · Limits

A weak first stage makes the root sensitive.

As the first-stage covariance shrinks, the moment line flattens. The same small vertical perturbation moves its root much farther.

Δθ=ΔME[Z~D~]\Delta\theta=\frac{\Delta M}{\mathbb E[\widetilde Z\widetilde D]}Δθ=E[ZD]ΔM​

Write the first-stage coefficient as π\piπ. The unperturbed mean score is π(2−θ)\pi(2-\theta)π(2−θ). A fixed moment perturbation of 0.08 shifts the root from 2 to 2+0.08/π2+0.08/\pi2+0.08/π. Scrolling reduces π\piπ from 1 to 0.08, moving the perturbed root from 2.08 to 3. This is a deterministic sensitivity calculation, not a simulated sampling distribution. At zero relevance, this moment does not identify the coefficient.

13 / 17 · Limits

Orthogonality does not repair an invalid instrument.

Give the instrument a direct effect on the outcome. The IV ratio now combines that direct effect with the treatment effect.

Y=2D+g0(X)+U+δV⟹θ^=2+δ/πY=2D+g_0(X)+U+\delta V\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\widehat\theta=2+\delta/\piY=2D+g0​(X)+U+δV⟹θ=2+δ/π

This scene changes the model by adding δV\delta VδV directly to the outcome, while the first stage remains π=1\pi=1π=1. As δ\deltaδ grows from 0 to 0.6, the IV ratio grows from 2 to 2.6. All nuisance functions remain correct. The score can still equal zero at the wrong causal value, so successful computation and good prediction do not establish exclusion.

14 / 17 · Limits

Inference also depends on instrument strength.

Score variation is divided by the squared first-stage moment. Ordinary DML standard errors require sufficiently strong identification.

se^(θ^)=n−1∑iψ^i2nJ^2,J^=n−1∑iZ~iD~i\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\widehat\theta)=\sqrt{\frac{n^{-1}\sum_i\widehat\psi_i^2}{n\widehat J^2}},\qquad \widehat J=n^{-1}\sum_i\widetilde Z_i\widetilde D_ise(θ)=nJ2n−1∑i​ψ​i2​​​,J=n−1i∑​Zi​Di​

This expression is for independent observations, evaluated at the estimated coefficient. Regular inference also requires moment bounds, consistent nuisance learning, and appropriate product-rate conditions, including ∥q^−q0∥2(∥ℓ^−ℓ0∥2+∥m^−m0∥2)=op(n−1/2)\|\widehat q-q_0\|_2(\|\widehat\ell-\ell_0\|_2+\|\widehat m-m_0\|_2)=o_p(n^{-1/2})∥q​−q0​∥2​(∥ℓ−ℓ0​∥2​+∥m−m0​∥2​)=op​(n−1/2), with the identifying moment bounded away from zero. Dependence requires an appropriate variance treatment. Weak-identification robust inference is a separate problem; cross-fitting does not make this ordinary normal approximation valid under weak instruments.

16 / 17 · A separate LATE extension

The ratio targets compliers.

The encouragement changes treatment by 0.5 and the outcome by 2. The Wald ratio is 4; the population ATE is 3.8.

θLATE=E[Y∣Z=1]−E[Y∣Z=0]E[D∣Z=1]−E[D∣Z=0]=4\theta_{\mathrm{LATE}}=\frac{\mathbb E[Y\mid Z=1]-\mathbb E[Y\mid Z=0]}{\mathbb E[D\mid Z=1]-\mathbb E[D\mid Z=0]}=4θLATE​=E[D∣Z=1]−E[D∣Z=0]E[Y∣Z=1]−E[Y∣Z=0]​=4

Under the stated binary-IV assumptions, the Wald ratio identifies E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣D(1)>D(0)]\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D(1)>D(0)]E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣D(1)>D(0)]. Always-takers and never-takers contribute no encouragement-induced change in treatment or outcome. Their treatment effects still contribute to the population ATE: 0.3(2)+0.5(4)+0.2(6)=3.80.3(2)+0.5(4)+0.2(6)=3.80.3(2)+0.5(4)+0.2(6)=3.8. Thus LATE and ATE differ in this example. No constant effect assumption is imposed in this extension.

17 / 17 · A separate LATE extension

Use two AIPW contrasts for conditional IV.

With covariate-dependent encouragement, build one adjusted encouragement contrast for the outcome and one for treatment. Their orthogonal ratio targets LATE under the conditional IV assumptions.
