Doubly robust estimation

How outcome predictions and treatment probabilities combine to estimate an average causal effect.

The example follows 16 constructed respondents receiving or not receiving civic information. Outcomes are political-knowledge scores. Treatment counts deliberately match the stated propensities; outcomes have no noise. The exact cancellations illustrate the arithmetic, not a claim of finite-sample unbiasedness in research data.

1 / 16 · The causal question

Two outcomes. One observation.

Each respondent has a knowledge score with and without the information. We observe the score under the treatment they actually received.

Yi=DiYi(1)+(1−Di)Yi(0)Y_i=D_iY_i(1)+(1-D_i)Y_i(0)Yi​=Di​Yi​(1)+(1−Di​)Yi​(0)

The treatment DDD is binary. The target is the population average treatment effect, τ=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]\tau=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)]τ=E[Y(1)−Y(0)]. Both potential outcomes are available in this constructed world, which lets us reveal the truth. In research data, one is missing for every respondent. The effect is 6 points in the low-engagement stratum and 2 in the high-engagement stratum. Equal population shares give an ATE of 4.

2 / 16 · The causal question

Treatment follows engagement.

High-engagement respondents are more likely to receive the information. They also have higher knowledge scores before treatment.

e(x)=Pr⁡(D=1∣X=x)e(x)=\Pr(D=1\mid X=x)e(x)=Pr(D=1∣X=x)

There are eight respondents in each stratum. Two low-engagement respondents and six high-engagement respondents receive treatment. The propensities are therefore 0.25 and 0.75 in this specially balanced example. To interpret adjusted contrasts causally, assume consistency, no interference, conditional exchangeability given XXX, and overlap. These assumptions concern the treatment and assignment process, not the quality of a prediction model.

3 / 16 · The causal question

Adjustment needs an identifying argument.

Assume well-defined treatment and no interference. Given engagement, assignment must be independent of potential outcomes, with both treatment states possible.

(Y(0),Y(1))⊥D∣X,0<e(X)<1(Y(0),Y(1))\perp D\mid X,\qquad 0<e(X)<1(Y(0),Y(1))⊥D∣X,0<e(X)<1

Consistency connects the observed treatment to its potential outcome. No interference means one respondent's outcome does not depend on another respondent's treatment. Conditional exchangeability says that, after conditioning on XXX, treatment assignment carries no further information about the potential outcomes. Overlap requires positive probability of both treatment states at each relevant covariate value. A more flexible predictor cannot establish these identifying conditions.

Observed-covariate adjustment requires these causal assumptions.

4 / 16 · The causal question

The observed gap is 13.

The treated average is 38; the control average is 25. Their difference mixes treatment effects with different starting points.

38−25=13butτ=438-25=13\qquad\text{but}\qquad\tau=438−25=13butτ=4

The treated mean is (2×26+6×42)/8=38(2\times26+6\times42)/8=38(2×26+6×42)/8=38. The control mean is (6×20+2×40)/8=25(6\times20+2\times40)/8=25(6×20+2×40)/8=25. The gap of 13 compares differently composed groups. The average effect is (6+2)/2=4(6+2)/2=4(6+2)/2=4. This discrepancy appears even though every outcome in the example is measured without noise.

5 / 16 · Two adjustment strategies

Predict both conditional outcomes.

Compare treatment and control predictions at the same engagement level, then average over the population.

τ^OR=1n∑i[μ^1(Xi)−μ^0(Xi)]\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{OR}}=\frac1n\sum_i[\widehat\mu_1(X_i)-\widehat\mu_0(X_i)]τOR​=n1​i∑​[μ​1​(Xi​)−μ​0​(Xi​)]

Define μd(x)=E[Y∣D=d,X=x]\mu_d(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid D=d,X=x]μd​(x)=E[Y∣D=d,X=x]. Under the identifying assumptions, their difference is the conditional average treatment effect. Here the two conditional contrasts are 26−20=626-20=626−20=6 and 42−40=242-40=242−40=2. Outcome regression averages predictions over the same covariate distribution for both treatment states. Its accuracy depends on estimating the relevant conditional outcome means.

6 / 16 · Two adjustment strategies

Reweight the observed outcomes.

A treated respondent who rarely receives treatment represents more people. The same logic applies to controls.

τ^IPW=1n∑i[DiYie^i−(1−Di)Yi1−e^i]\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{IPW}}=\frac1n\sum_i\left[\frac{D_iY_i}{\widehat e_i}-\frac{(1-D_i)Y_i}{1-\widehat e_i}\right]τIPW​=n1​i∑​[ei​Di​Yi​​−1−ei​(1−Di​)Yi​​]

Write e^i=e^(Xi)\widehat e_i=\widehat e(X_i)ei​=e(Xi​). A treated low-engagement respondent receives weight 4; a treated high-engagement respondent receives weight 4/34/34/3. The control weights reverse that pattern. With the true propensities, the weighted potential-outcome means are 34 and 30, so the estimated contrast is 4. 7 / 16 · Build AIPW

A prediction can be corrected.

Suppose the outcome models predict 28 under control and 30 under treatment for everyone. The observed prediction errors reveal what those models miss.

residual=Yi−μ^Di(Xi)\text{residual}=Y_i-\widehat\mu_{D_i}(X_i)residual=Yi​−μ​Di​​(Xi​)

These deliberately incorrect outcome models produce a plug-in contrast of 2. A low-engagement treated respondent has residual 26−30=−426-30=-426−30=−4. A high-engagement control respondent has residual 40−28=1240-28=1240−28=12. AIPW scales the observed residual by the probability of that respondent's observed treatment state, then adds or subtracts it from the predicted contrast.

Flat outcome models are deliberately misspecified.

Add the weighted correction.

For a low-engagement treated respondent: a predicted contrast of 2, plus a residual of −4 weighted by 4, gives a contribution of −14.

9 / 16 · Build AIPW

Average the contributions.

The individual corrections can be large. Across this constructed population, they correct the predicted contrast from 2 to 4.

τ^AIPW=1n∑iϕ^i\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{AIPW}}=\frac1n\sum_i\widehat\phi_iτAIPW​=n1​i∑​ϕ​i​

There are two contributions of −14-14−14, six of 38/338/338/3, six of 18, and two of −46-46−46. Their sum is 64, giving an average of 4. The propensity is correct while the outcome models are incorrect. The exact result relies on the specially balanced, noiseless construction; estimated nuisances and random samples generally produce sampling error.

10 / 16 · Double robustness

Both parts are correct.

With the true conditional outcome means, the residuals are zero in this noiseless example. Averaging the predicted contrasts gives 4.

τ^=4\widehat\tau=4τ=4

Double robustness concerns two groups of nuisance functions: the propensity score and the pair of conditional outcome means. At their true values, the AIPW population expectation equals the ATE. In this constructed data set the sample average also equals it exactly.

11 / 16 · Double robustness

The outcome models carry the estimate.

Replace the propensity with 0.5 for everyone. The correct outcome means still give an average contribution of 4.

μ^d=μd,e^≠e\widehat\mu_d=\mu_d,\quad\widehat e\ne eμ​d​=μd​,e=e

With correct conditional outcome means, the residual has conditional expectation zero. Multiplying that residual by a bounded function of treatment and covariates does not change that expectation. AIPW can therefore remain consistent despite an incorrect propensity model, subject to regularity conditions. Both treatment-specific outcome means are included in this statement.

12 / 16 · Double robustness

The propensity carries the correction.

Restore the true propensity, then return to the incorrect flat outcome models. Weighted residual corrections recover the average effect.

μ^d≠μd,e^=e\widehat\mu_d\ne\mu_d,\quad\widehat e=eμ​d​=μd​,e=e

When the propensity is correct, weighting the observed residuals reconstructs the average prediction error under each treatment state. The correction cancels the average error in the outcome-model contrast. This is a statement about the expectation and, under suitable conditions, consistency. The finite-sample cancellation here is a deliberate feature of the example.

13 / 16 · Double robustness

Both can miss the target.

Use the flat outcome models and a propensity of 0.5. The AIPW estimate returns to 13 in this example.

μ^d≠μd,e^≠e\widehat\mu_d\ne\mu_d,\quad\widehat e\ne eμ​d​=μd​,e=e

The two estimation routes do not guarantee protection when both are misspecified. Here the AIPW expression simplifies to the original difference in group means. It gives 13. Some errors can happen to cancel, but double robustness makes no general promise in this case. It also does not address an omitted confounder that invalidates conditional exchangeability.

14 / 16 · From AIPW to DML

Overlap controls the correction size.

As a treatment becomes unlikely, its inverse-probability weight grows. Small prediction errors can then create large corrections.

e(x):  0.25⟶0.041e(x):  4⟶25e(x):\;0.25\longrightarrow0.04\qquad\frac1{e(x)}:\;4\longrightarrow25e(x):0.25⟶0.04e(x)1​:4⟶25

This is a separate weight illustration, not a change to the 16-person calculation. When a propensity approaches zero or one, few comparable observations support one of the treatment states. Double robustness does not supply missing information. Trimming observations changes the target population, while clipping weights changes the calculation; neither operation is silently applied here.

15 / 16 · From AIPW to DML

Predict each fold from the others.

Fit the nuisance models outside the highlighted fold. Predict its observations, compute their contributions, then rotate the held-out fold.

ϕ^i=ϕ(Wi;η^−k(i))\widehat\phi_i=\phi(W_i;\widehat\eta_{-k(i)})ϕ​i​=ϕ(Wi​;η​−k(i)​)

Partition the sample into folds. For every fold, fit the two outcome models and the propensity using only the other folds, including their tuning and preprocessing. Use those fits to predict the held-out observations. Pool all held-out contributions and average them once. 16 / 16 · From AIPW to DML

Consistency and inference are different claims.

An orthogonal score and cross-fitting support inference when nuisance errors converge sufficiently fast and the sampling and overlap conditions hold.

se^(τ^)=1n2∑i(ϕ^i−τ^)2\widehat{\mathrm{se}}(\widehat\tau)=\sqrt{\frac1{n^2}\sum_i(\widehat\phi_i-\widehat\tau)^2}se(τ)=n21​i∑​(ϕ​i​−τ)2​

Under i.i.d. sampling and appropriate regularity conditions, the estimated influence-function variance gives a standard error. One common sufficient rate condition requires the product of the propensity error and outcome-regression error in L2L_2L2​ norm to be op(n−1/2)o_p(n^{-1/2})op​(n−1/2), with both errors also vanishing and adequate overlap. Double-robust consistency alone does not establish this inference result. Clustered data require a sampling and variance treatment appropriate to the clusters.

Cross-fitted AIPW is an application of the DML framework.
