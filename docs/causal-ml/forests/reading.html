Causal forests

Follow honest tree leaves into forest weights, then use those weights to estimate a conditional treatment effect.

All numerical examples are constructed. There are 240 observations in 15 covariate cells, with known nuisance functions and balanced outcome noise. Three explicitly specified partitions illustrate forest-weight arithmetic; they are not a trained production forest. The split comparison is an actual gradient calculation. Honesty and evaluation diagrams describe data flow. No research confidence intervals are reported. The estimator shown is the residualized, local-moment version of a causal forest in the generalized random forest framework.

5 / 19 · Build the partitions

Separate choosing a partition from estimating within it.

For each tree, one part of its subsample chooses the splits. Another part supplies the observations in its prediction leaves.

Sb∩Ib=∅S_b\cap I_b=\varnothingSb​∩Ib​=∅

Honesty separates the splitting set SbS_bSb​ from the estimation set IbI_bIb​ within a tree. Route the estimation observations through the chosen partition, then use their outcomes for local estimation. A person may have different roles in different trees. Empty estimation leaves need an explicit handling rule; treatment-information requirements also matter. Honesty addresses adaptive reuse of outcomes. It does not itself establish unconfoundedness, nuisance accuracy, or negligible neighborhood bias. This diagram is a data-flow schematic, not a claim that the balanced construction is an independent random sample.

The prior-knowledge coordinate stays at 0.5 while engagement moves from 0.1 to 0.9. These three shallow partitions produce a stepwise effect estimate. The neighborhood is learned for an estimation task; it need not match Euclidean nearest neighbors. 10 / 19 · Estimate the local effect

Keep the local intercept after residualization.

Global residuals need not have zero mean in a forest neighborhood. Center both residuals using the local forest weights.

Di∘=D~i−D‾α,Yi∘=Y~i−Y‾αD_i^\circ=\widetilde D_i-\overline D_\alpha,\qquad Y_i^\circ=\widetilde Y_i-\overline Y_\alphaDi∘​=Di​−Dα​,Yi∘​=Yi​−Yα​

The weighted means are D‾α=∑iαiD~i\overline D_\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i\widetilde D_iDα​=∑i​αi​Di​ and Y‾α=∑iαiY~i\overline Y_\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i\widetilde Y_iYα​=∑i​αi​Yi​. To expose their role, this separate algebra check shifts every treatment residual by −0.15 and every outcome residual by −0.8. A through-origin slope becomes about 4.578. Centering within the neighborhood gives the original slope, about 4.501, with intercept about −0.125 before centering. Constant offsets are absorbed by an intercept; this does not establish robustness to arbitrary nuisance errors that vary with covariates.

Pool the moments before solving.

The three local tree slopes average to about 4.517. Solving the pooled forest regression gives about 4.501.

For this balanced example, each selected leaf has zero residual means. Its normalized covariance and variance can therefore be averaged directly before taking their ratio. The tree slopes are about 4.726, 5.156, and 3.669; their ordinary average is about 4.517. The pooled covariance divided by the pooled variance is about 4.501. In general, also pool the first moments: differing local means contribute to the pooled covariance. A smaller neighborhood changes bias and information.

For a query at engagement 0.3, narrow a simple window. The local slope approaches 4.8 while the number of observations falls from 240 to 48.

This separate window calculation uses known nuisance functions, uniformly weighted observations inside AAA, and a population interpretation of the balanced construction. The broad neighborhood gives slope 4; successively narrower windows give about 4.331, 4.726, and 4.8. The last window retains only engagement 0.3, where the effect is constant across prior knowledge. A broad residual regression generally averages effects with treatment-variance weights. Smaller neighborhoods can reduce approximation bias but leave less information; this noiseless conditional cancellation does not measure that sampling-variance tradeoff or promise monotone improvement in real data.

14 / 19 · Understand the approximation

Weight concentration is not treatment information.

Even a well-populated neighborhood can have little treatment variation. Near-deterministic treatment makes the local effect loss flat.

The sensitivity plot fixes a conditional effect of 4.8 and compares local excess residual losses e(1−e)(t−4.8)2e(1-e)(t-4.8)^2e(1−e)(t−4.8)2. As eee falls from 0.5 to 0.02, the coefficient falls from 0.25 to 0.0196. A large effective sample size based on forest weights alone cannot rule out this weak local information. Local overlap, treatment counts, and residual variation deserve separate checks. This is an analytic sensitivity calculation; no estimated confidence interval is attached to it.

16 / 19 · Separate the sources of uncertainty

Data separations do different jobs.

Honesty separates split selection from estimation. Out-of-bag prediction leaves a person out of both tree roles. Nuisance fitting and final evaluation need their own separation.

i is OOB for tree b  ⟺  i∉Sb∪Ibi\text{ is OOB for tree }b\iff i\notin S_b\cup I_bi is OOB for tree b⟺i∈/Sb​∪Ib​

The schematic tracks a focal person through several trees. A person used only in a tree's estimation sample is still in bag; their outcome contributes to its prediction. An OOB prediction uses only trees omitting that person from the entire tree subsample. Nuisance cross-fitting separately prevents direct in-sample nuisance predictions. Held-out evaluation assesses the chosen effect-learning procedure, including tuning and selection. OOB predictions can be useful, but repeated model selection or subgroup discovery on the same outcomes does not leave an untouched evaluation sample.

17 / 19 · Separate the sources of uncertainty

Tree dispersion is not a sampling standard error.

Copying the same three trees ten times leaves every forest weight and prediction unchanged. A formula that treats those copies as independent would shrink anyway.

se^{τ^(x)}≠sd⁡(τ^1(x),…,τ^B(x))/B\widehat{\mathrm{se}}\{\widehat\tau(x)\}\ne\operatorname{sd}(\widehat\tau_1(x),\ldots,\widehat\tau_B(x))/\sqrt Bse{τ(x)}=sd(τ1​(x),…,τB​(x))/B​

The displayed shrinking quantity is the population standard deviation of the three local tree slopes divided by the square root of the number of copied entries. It is deliberately invalid as a sampling standard error. Appropriate forest inference accounts for reuse of observations and the local estimating equation; honest subsampling, regularity, overlap, nuisance conditions, and control of localization bias matter. Original causal-forest theory and generalized random forest theory provide different variance constructions. A pointwise interval at a prespecified xxx is not a simultaneous band over a searched effect surface. This lesson reports no research interval from three specified partitions.

18 / 19 · Evaluate and summarize

Evaluate heterogeneity without observing individual effects.

Keep effect predictions fixed before examining evaluation outcomes. Compare groups or residual losses with procedures suited to the scientific question.

E[ϕ(W;η0)∣G(X)]=E[τ(X)∣G(X)]\mathbb E[\phi(W;\eta_0)\mid G(X)]=\mathbb E[\tau(X)\mid G(X)]E[ϕ(W;η0​)∣G(X)]=E[τ(X)∣G(X)]

Individual causal effects are unobserved. One approach is to evaluate prespecified groups using held-out doubly robust scores; residual loss and calibration address related but distinct questions. In this known-truth illustration, the two lower engagement values have average effect 5.2, the middle value has effect 4, and the two higher values have average effect 2.8. The animation assembles these constructed group means; it is not a validation result for a fitted ranking. For groups learned from data, separate group construction from effect evaluation and use inference appropriate to the resulting target and selection procedure.

19 / 19 · Evaluate and summarize

An average effect needs its own estimating step.

Averaging this forest’s predictions gives about 4.011. AIPW corrections recover 4 in the construction when the propensity is correct.

The fifteen covariate cells have equal probability, so the true ATE is 4. The average prediction from the three specified partitions is 4.010822.
