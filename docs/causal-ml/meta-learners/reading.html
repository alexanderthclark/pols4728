Meta-learners: S, T, and X

Turn outcome learners into conditional-effect learners, and see how sharing data, separating treatment arms, and imputing contrasts change the problem.

The shared example contains 80 constructed observations at five engagement values. Each value has 16 observations; treatment counts match the stated propensities and outcome noise is balanced within each treatment arm. The conditional effect is 6−4x. Small least-squares and penalized least-squares fits are actually calculated; they illustrate learning architectures, not a ranking of methods on research data. Later sensitivity examples are identified separately.

1 / 16 · The target

Learn how the average effect changes with covariates.

Information may help respondents with low prior engagement more than those with high engagement. The target is a function of engagement.

τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]\tau(x)=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]τ(x)=E[Y(1)−Y(0)∣X=x]

Here DDD is receipt of civic information, YYY is a political-knowledge score, and XXX is prior engagement on a zero-to-one scale. We construct μ0(x)=20+8x+4x2\mu_0(x)=20+8x+4x^2μ0​(x)=20+8x+4x2 and μ1(x)=μ0(x)+6−4x\mu_1(x)=\mu_0(x)+6-4xμ1​(x)=μ0​(x)+6−4x. Thus the CATE is τ(x)=6−4x\tau(x)=6-4xτ(x)=6−4x, with population average 4 over five equally common engagement values. This function describes an average at a covariate value. It is not generally an identified individual treatment effect. Repeated observations can occupy the same plotted position.

2 / 16 · The target

The architecture does not identify the effect.

Treatment becomes more common as engagement increases. Interpreting conditional outcome contrasts causally requires exchangeability and overlap, alongside consistency and no interference.

(Y(0),Y(1))⊥D∣X,0<e(x)<1(Y(0),Y(1))\perp D\mid X,\qquad 0<e(x)<1(Y(0),Y(1))⊥D∣X,0<e(x)<1

The propensity is e(x)=0.25+0.5xe(x)=0.25+0.5xe(x)=0.25+0.5x. Treatment counts are 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 out of 16 at the five covariate values. The remaining observations are controls. Under the stated assumptions, τ(x)=μ1(x)−μ0(x)\tau(x)=\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)τ(x)=μ1​(x)−μ0​(x), where μd(x)=E[Y∣D=d,X=x]\mu_d(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid D=d,X=x]μd​(x)=E[Y∣D=d,X=x]. 3 / 16 · S-learner

Give one outcome learner the treatment indicator.

The S-learner uses all observations to predict outcomes from covariates and treatment. One fitted function supplies both treatment-state predictions.

f^(x,d)≈E[Y∣X=x,D=d]\widehat f(x,d)\approx\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x,D=d]f​(x,d)≈E[Y∣X=x,D=d]

The single learner takes (X,D)(X,D)(X,D) as inputs. Its architecture determines how much information is shared between treatment states and whether treatment interactions can be learned. Our transparent fitted example uses the features 1,x,x2,d,dx1,x,x^2,d,dx1,x,x2,d,dx. Least squares on the 80 observations recovers the two true conditional means because the functions belong to this class and noise is balanced. That exact recovery is a property of the construction, not a general guarantee.

4 / 16 · S-learner

Predict twice at the same covariate value.

Hold engagement fixed. Change only the treatment input from control to treatment, then subtract the two predictions.

τ^S(x)=f^(x,1)−f^(x,0)\widehat\tau_S(x)=\widehat f(x,1)-\widehat f(x,0)τS​(x)=f​(x,1)−f​(x,0)

At engagement 0.25, the fitted control mean is 22.25 and the fitted treatment mean is 27.25, giving a contrast of 5. These are two predictions for the same covariates, not a difference between respondents at different engagement levels. The model can share a complicated baseline pattern across treatment states while representing the effect with a simpler interaction.

At x = 0.25: 27.25 − 22.25 = 5

5 / 16 · S-learner

An additive treatment term forces a constant effect.

Remove treatment interactions from this fitted example. The S-learner now predicts an effect of 4 at every engagement value.

f^(x,d)=h^(x)+β^d⟹τ^S(x)=β^\widehat f(x,d)=\widehat h(x)+\widehat\beta d\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\widehat\tau_S(x)=\widehat\betaf​(x,d)=h(x)+β​d⟹τS​(x)=β​

Refitting with features 1,x,x2,d1,x,x^2,d1,x,x2,d gives the treatment coefficient 4. A treatment indicator alone does not ensure heterogeneous effect predictions: an additive architecture makes the contrast constant by construction. Other learners can learn interactions without explicitly supplied product terms. The lesson is to examine the function class, rather than infer flexibility from the S-learner label.

6 / 16 · S-learner

Outcome regularization can distort the contrast.

Penalize the treatment terms in the flexible S-model. Outcome MSE rises from 1 to 1.10, while the estimated low-engagement effect falls from 6 to 4.85.

1n∑i[Yi−f(Xi,Di)]2+λ(βD2+βDX2)\frac1n\sum_i[Y_i-f(X_i,D_i)]^2+\lambda(\beta_D^2+\beta_{DX}^2)n1​i∑​[Yi​−f(Xi​,Di​)]2+λ(βD2​+βDX2​)

This sensitivity calculation penalizes only the coefficients of DDD and DXDXDX while retaining the same feature class. Scrolling increases λ\lambdaλ from 0 to 0.01. The resulting low- and high-engagement effects are approximately 4.85 and 2.71. Small changes in overall outcome-prediction error can accompany meaningful changes in a treatment contrast. This demonstrates one possible regularization effect, not a general claim that S-learners always shrink effects or perform poorly.

7 / 16 · T-learner

Fit the two outcome surfaces separately.

Train one learner among controls and another among treated respondents. Each arm can have its own outcome model.

μ^0(x),μ^1(x)\widehat\mu_0(x),\qquad\widehat\mu_1(x)μ​0​(x),μ​1​(x)

The two learners use disjoint treatment arms. They need not have identical architectures or tuning choices, although those choices still require appropriate validation. The toy example fits one quadratic function per arm. Both recover the true conditional means exactly because the construction balances noise at every covariate value. Separate fits offer flexibility but give each learner fewer observations and do not automatically share baseline structure.

8 / 16 · T-learner

Subtract the fitted surfaces.

The vertical gap between the two outcome functions becomes an effect curve. Here the gap decreases linearly even though both outcome curves are nonlinear.

τ^T(x)=μ^1(x)−μ^0(x)\widehat\tau_T(x)=\widehat\mu_1(x)-\widehat\mu_0(x)τT​(x)=μ​1​(x)−μ​0​(x)

The T-learner estimates two response surfaces and subtracts them at the same xxx. In this exact example, subtracting cancels the shared quadratic baseline and leaves 6−4x6-4x6−4x. In less favorable data, predicting two complicated functions well enough to estimate their difference can be harder than directly learning a simpler effect function. This observation motivates the additional regression stage in the X-learner.

The fitted effect is a difference of conditional means.

9 / 16 · T-learner

Errors in two fits can reinforce each other.

Give the two outcome fits small errors in opposite directions. Their difference inherits both errors.

τ^T(x)−τ(x)=δ1(x)−δ0(x)\widehat\tau_T(x)-\tau(x)=\delta_1(x)-\delta_0(x)τT​(x)−τ(x)=δ1​(x)−δ0​(x)

This separate sensitivity scene sets δ0(x)=0.6sin⁡(2πx)\delta_0(x)=0.6\sin(2\pi x)δ0​(x)=0.6sin(2πx) and δ1(x)=−0.6sin⁡(2πx)\delta_1(x)=-0.6\sin(2\pi x)δ1​(x)=−0.6sin(2πx). Each outcome error is at most 0.6, but the effect error can reach 1.2. These perturbations are specified, not estimated from the main data. Correlated errors can also cancel; the point is that outcome accuracy alone does not determine the accuracy of their difference.

10 / 16 · X-learner

Impute a contrast for each treated observation.

For a treated respondent, compare the observed outcome with the predicted control outcome. The resulting target is noisy.

Δ^i 1=Yi−μ^0(Xi),Di=1\widehat\Delta_i^{\,1}=Y_i-\widehat\mu_0(X_i),\qquad D_i=1Δi1​=Yi​−μ​0​(Xi​),Di​=1

The first stage estimates the two outcome regressions as in a T-learner. For treated observations, subtract the control prediction from the observed outcome. With a correct control regression, the conditional mean of this imputed contrast is the CATE. In our example it equals τ(Xi)+εi\tau(X_i)+\varepsilon_iτ(Xi​)+εi​. It is not an observed individual causal effect, because the individual's untreated outcome remains missing.

Treated-arm imputation uses the control outcome model.

11 / 16 · X-learner

Reverse the subtraction for controls.

For a control respondent, compare the predicted treated outcome with the observed control outcome. The sign is important.

Δ^i 0=μ^1(Xi)−Yi,Di=0\widehat\Delta_i^{\,0}=\widehat\mu_1(X_i)-Y_i,\qquad D_i=0Δi0​=μ​1​(Xi​)−Yi​,Di​=0

The control-arm imputed contrast uses the treated outcome regression. With a correct treated regression, its conditional mean is also τ(x)\tau(x)τ(x). Here it equals τ(Xi)−εi\tau(X_i)-\varepsilon_iτ(Xi​)−εi​. Both arms therefore generate noisy targets for the same effect function, but each depends on the quality of the opposite arm's outcome model.

12 / 16 · X-learner

Regress the imputed contrasts on covariates.

Learn one effect function from treated pseudo-outcomes and another from control pseudo-outcomes. These regressions can use a simpler function class.

τ^1(x)←Δ^ 1,τ^0(x)←Δ^ 0\widehat\tau_1(x)\leftarrow\widehat\Delta^{\,1},\qquad\widehat\tau_0(x)\leftarrow\widehat\Delta^{\,0}τ1​(x)←Δ1,τ0​(x)←Δ0

The subscripts index the arm used for the effect regression: τ^1\widehat\tau_1τ1​ is learned among treated observations, and τ^0\widehat\tau_0τ0​ among controls. Our second-stage linear fits both recover 6−4x6-4x6−4x, even though the first-stage response functions are quadratic. This exact result uses the constructed balance and correct first-stage fits. More generally, second-stage estimation and approximation errors remain.

13 / 16 · X-learner

Combine the two effect fits with explicit weights.

In a separate illustration, the two fits predict 6 and 5.2. Changing their weights from one half to 0.2 and 0.8 changes the blend from 5.6 to 5.36.

τ^X(x)=g(x)τ^0(x)+[1−g(x)]τ^1(x)\widehat\tau_X(x)=g(x)\widehat\tau_0(x)+[1-g(x)]\widehat\tau_1(x)τX​(x)=g(x)τ0​(x)+[1−g(x)]τ1​(x)

A common choice is g(x)=e^(x)g(x)=\widehat e(x)g(x)=e(x). Under this convention, the propensity multiplies the control-arm effect fit τ^0\widehat\tau_0τ0​, not the treated-arm effect fit τ^1\widehat\tau_1τ1​. The numerical inputs 6 and 5.2 are deliberately imperfect, specified estimates at a location whose true effect is 5. The propensity blend is one choice, not a universally optimal weight and not a doubly robust correction.

14 / 16 · X-learner

Abundant controls can help the treated-arm effect fit.

With many controls, the control outcome model may be estimated well. That model supplies the missing comparison for treated respondents.

e(x) small⟹1−e(x) largee(x)\ \text{small}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad1-e(x)\ \text{large}e(x) small⟹1−e(x) large

The diagram uses a schematic population of 80 controls and 20 treated observations. A well-estimated μ0\mu_0μ0​ can make treated pseudo-outcomes useful for learning a simple τ1\tau_1τ1​, which receives weight 1−e1-e1−e. This helps explain the X-learner's behavior in some imbalanced settings. Counts alone do not establish prediction accuracy: local support, noise, effect complexity, and the base learners all matter. Imbalance does not remove the need for overlap.

15 / 16 · Compare architectures

A simple effect can sit between complicated outcomes.

The two outcome curves share a nonlinear baseline. Their difference is linear. Different architectures can exploit that structure differently.

μ1(x)−μ0(x)=6−4x\mu_1(x)-\mu_0(x)=6-4xμ1​(x)−μ0​(x)=6−4x

An S-learner may share the baseline directly; a T-learner estimates each response surface; an X-learner adds regressions aimed at the effect structure. Any of them can succeed on the main constructed example. There is no universal ranking from their labels. The fitted function classes, regularization, sample sizes, and covariate support determine the learning problem.

S: one response model · T: two response models · X: impute, then regress

16 / 16 · Compare architectures

Effect evaluation needs its own argument.

An outcome can be checked against its observed value. An individual treatment effect cannot. Reserve data to evaluate the entire effect-learning procedure.

Yi(1)−Yi(0) is not observedY_i(1)-Y_i(0)\ \text{is not observed}Yi​(1)−Yi​(0) is not observed

The known effect curve lets us check calculations in this constructed world. Real data do not supply individual effect labels. Suitable held-out criteria can use orthogonal scores or residual losses, while randomized data can support prespecified group-effect checks. Prediction MSE for outcomes alone is insufficient. If nuisance models are cross-fitted, that still does not turn observations used to fit the final effect function into an untouched evaluation set.
