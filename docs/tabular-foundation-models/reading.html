Tabular foundation models

Following Christoph Molnar’s book and the Nature 2025 TabPFN paper. The opening compares measured predictions on an American Time Use Survey task, including a balanced 200-case training sample. The lesson then follows the bear-survival task through a real miniature PFN training run, its actual inference calculations, an exact Bayesian illustration, and a measured XGBoost diagnostic. The proposed explanation for TabPFN’s behavior is identified as a hypothesis.

The data-generating process

This is the true data-generating process.

Bear type and strategy determine the probability of survival. Each simulated outcome is a random draw using the probability at its leaf.

This tree specifies the true conditional outcome distribution in our simulation. A brown bear with a fight response has survival probability logistic(−1) ≈ 26.9%; a brown bear with a lie-down response has logistic(1) ≈ 73.1%. For black bears, those probabilities reverse: fight gives 73.1%, and lie down gives 26.9%. Bear type and strategy are independent balanced binary variables, so the four leaves are equally likely. Freeze the weights. Supply a new context.

The same trained network returns 74.0% for context A and 23.6% for context B. Its intermediate calculations change because the input changes.

This is in-context learning. The function computed by the trained network is fixed, but it is a function of both the query and the labeled context. Replacing the context changes the row vectors, attention computations, pooled representation, and final prediction. In ordinary task-specific neural-network training, fitting a new dataset changes weights; here, pretraining has already learned a way to use the dataset as input. These particular predictions are imperfect: their exact Bayesian targets are approximately 72.3% and 27.7%. Learning the inference procedure does not guarantee exact inference or calibration.

An actual prediction

Decode that representation into a probability.

Stack the 32-number query vector and the 32-number attention output into 64 inputs. The decoder’s logit 1.047 becomes 74.0% survival through sigmoid.

The saved model concatenates the 32-dimensional query representation h* first and the 32-dimensional attention output c second. Thus [h*;c] is a 64-dimensional vector; the two vectors are not added coordinate by coordinate. A hidden ReLU layer followed by a scalar output produces the logit; sigmoid maps it into (0,1). During pretraining, query-label error could flow backward through this full computation. During inference, there is only the forward calculation. The network was trained to predict outcomes; it was not trained to reproduce explicit posterior weights. Under the task generator, the log-loss-optimal answer is nevertheless the posterior predictive probability.

The exact Bayesian target

The two deaths favor A too.

A predicts low survival for Black + Lie down and Brown + Fight. Their observed deaths therefore support A. All four observed outcomes are more likely under A.

Under A, each displayed outcome has probability p: survival in the two matching pairs, and death in the two mismatching pairs. Under B, each has probability 1 − p. The exact Bayesian target

Why does log loss favor the Bayesian answer?

For this context and query, the next outcome is Bernoulli(r), with r = 72.3%. Expected log loss is minimized by predicting q = r. Pretraining learns this mapping across its task distribution.

Let r = p(Y*=1 | x*,D), the posterior predictive probability induced by the task generator. Conditional expected binary log loss is −r log(q) − (1 − r) log(1 − q). Its derivative is (q − r)/(q(1 − q)), which is negative below r and positive above it. Equivalently, the loss is the binary entropy H(r) plus KL(Bernoulli(r) || Bernoulli(q)); the extra term is minimized at zero when q = r. For the displayed A-pattern context, r is approximately 0.7227468. The moving square is a candidate prediction q, not a recorded neural training trajectory. A single observed survival would favor q near one, but averaging the training objective over random query outcomes favors r. Averaging again over tasks makes the desired function the posterior predictive mapping for that prior. This characterization does not guarantee that a finite network or optimizer finds the population optimum, or that its pretraining prior matches a new real dataset.

Combine features, then compare encounters.

Feature attention combines columns within each encounter. Sample attention then compares corresponding representations across encounters. The query reads labeled context while its own survival outcome stays hidden.

Figure 1 describes a two-dimensional table transformer. Feature attention mixes the representations within an example, and sample attention exchanges information across examples for the same feature or feature group. Training rows attend to training rows. Test queries read training context; they do not send their unknown labels into it or attend to other test queries. The diagram traces one query’s incoming context paths, with its target withheld throughout. These operations recur across transformer layers. Why the interaction matters

A small tree can express the rule.

Split on bear type, then on strategy. The second split reveals the signal, but the first split alone leaves both groups at 50%.

A depth-two tree can represent the four conditional probabilities exactly. XGBoost constructs its trees through greedy split selection, which evaluates immediate improvement rather than the benefit of a subsequent pair of splits. At a constant initial prediction, neither signal feature offers a population-level gain. This is a difficulty of discovering the interaction, not a claim that trees cannot represent interactions. Sampling variation, deeper trees, and later boosting rounds can help.

A representable tree is not necessarily the tree found by greedy training. Sample imbalance can provide a useful first split.

Why the interaction matters

Noise offers more places to look.

A binary feature has one possible cut. A continuous distractor offers many thresholds, giving the split search more opportunities to find an accidental training improvement.

All irrelevant features have zero population predictive value, but their empirical split gains need not be zero. Maximizing over many candidate cutpoints can select a chance association. The useful binary variables have only one possible partition each. This combines with the interaction’s absent marginal signal to make continuous distractors particularly troublesome. Why the interaction matters

Does TabPFN solve the harder task?

The Nature paper trains on tasks with irrelevant predictors and tests robustness to shuffled-feature distractors. That motivates the bear comparison: can the model retain the joint signal while ignoring noise?

The XGBoost results diagnose unstable split search and show that particular changes repair it. The miniature PFN only learned to distinguish two rules on fixed relevant columns. The Nature 2025 paper supplies a more specific reason to test the harder problem: its synthetic generator can contain disconnected graph components that produce uninformative features, and Fig. 5 evaluates robustness by adding randomly shuffled copies of original features. Our independent Uniform(0,1) distractors are a different intervention. In the bear task, B and L each have no marginal association with Y, while their combination is predictive. Handling irrelevant columns must therefore allow useful combinations to survive; univariate screening would miss the signal. Pretraining and table attention make joint-pattern inference plausible, but they do not establish that a particular checkpoint succeeds here or identify its internal algorithm. Run that checkpoint and XGBoost on identical datasets, splits, and metrics. The diagram gives the predictor all columns without labeling which ones matter.

Using one in research

The interface is familiar. The work has moved.

In the standard in-context workflow, .fit() prepares the training context; .predict() uses that context to compute predictions. The pretrained weights remain fixed.

A scikit-learn-style interface hides the change in computational workflow. Loading weights, preparing data, and building caches can occur at fit time. Prediction uses the labeled context or its cached representation. The Nature 2025 default averages four classification or eight regression predictions from the same pretrained model with different data-processing variants. Feature-order shuffles and, for classification, class-label permutations are part of this pipeline. The model is not fully invariant to feature order. Each forward pass holds the weights fixed; an ensemble can require several passes. Caching reuses context computation across queries. These are the paper’s settings, not a promise about every later software version.

Using one in research

Keep the test labels outside the context.

Compare with a regularized model and boosted trees on the same held-out task. In cross-validation, each fold gets its own training context.

A pretrained predictor still needs an honest assessment on your problem. Never include validation or test outcomes in its labeled context, including through preprocessing. When selecting models or settings, keep final evaluation separate from selection. The Nature 2025 user guide notes that tree-based models can have an advantage on larger datasets and highly non-smooth regression problems. The paper’s main benchmark includes 29 classification and 28 regression datasets, each with at most 10,000 rows, 500 features, and 10 classes. Those are evaluated conditions for that model, not universal limits on today’s tabular foundation models. Compare accuracy, calibration, runtime, and memory on the task you actually need.

What the Nature 2025 result adds

Hollmann and colleagues’ Accurate predictions on small data with a tabular foundation model studies a much broader predictor than our miniature PFN. Use the open full text to connect these parts of the lesson to the evidence.

Paper sectionWhat to take from it
Figures 1–2; Methods: prior and architectureA distribution over synthetic data-generating processes supplies varied prediction problems. Attention combines information within rows and across labeled examples. The same trained network handles new contexts.
Figure 5a: sample efficiencyTabPFN using half the training samples matches default CatBoost’s aggregate performance in this comparison. This is a benchmark summary, not a guarantee for each dataset or a claim that half as many observations always suffice.
Figure 5a: irrelevant featuresAdding shuffled copies of existing columns tests robustness to distractors. The bear task adds independent uniform noise instead; a matched experiment must test whether the benefit transfers.
Methods: inference detailsThe default pipeline averages four classification or eight regression variants with different processing. Optional pair products are disabled by default. Fixed network weights do not imply zero preprocessing, one total forward pass, or no useful settings to compare.

The main benchmark contains 29 classification and 28 regression datasets with at most 10,000 observations, 500 features, and 10 classes. Evaluation repeats random 90/10 train–test splits for ten seeds. The baselines include XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, random forests, linear models, SVMs, an MLP, and AutoGluon. Baseline tuning uses five-fold cross-validation and time budgets up to four hours. In figures that normalize predictive performance, the best and worst performance on each dataset anchor the scale; a normalized difference is not an AUC difference.

The speed comparisons measure work on a new dataset. They exclude the large shared pretraining cost: the reported run trained on roughly 130 million synthetic datasets using eight RTX 2080 Ti GPUs for about two weeks. Benchmark algorithms received eight CPU cores, and TabPFN additionally used an RTX 2080 Ti GPU. These conditions matter when interpreting its few-second default results against tuned baselines.

The paper also extends beyond classification. Its regression head represents a distribution over possible target values, including distributions with more than one mode, from which means and quantiles can be obtained. That capability does not guarantee calibration on a new research population. The user guide identifies larger datasets and highly non-smooth regression functions as settings where tree-based methods may have an advantage.

The central explanation is statistical reuse: pretraining spends computation learning how evidence in a small table changes predictions under a broad task prior. A suitable prior can reduce how much must be learned from that table alone. Prior mismatch and approximation error remain, as our two-world example makes visible. None of the paper’s aggregate comparisons establishes why a particular checkpoint would outperform XGBoost on the bear interaction.
