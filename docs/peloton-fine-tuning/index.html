Fine-tuning an encoder

Carry the argument with you

  • The labeling scheme defines what the classifier learns to reproduce.
  • Full fine-tuning updates the pretrained encoder as well as the task-specific head.
  • Cross-entropy connects a target label to a gradient update through both parts of the model.
  • A stratified validation set measures agreement on reserved sentences; selecting a checkpoint with it makes it part of model selection.
  • An embedding picture can suggest structure without establishing held-out performance or validating the substantive interpretation.

The workflow is based on the user-supplied fine_tuning_distilbert.py: Gemini Flash labeling, a stratified 80/20 split, a pretrained DistilBERT sequence classifier, four training epochs, and validation after each epoch. 1. What does an instructor ask of you?

Tara Isabella Burton’s reading of modern fitness culture treats self-love and relentless self-improvement as moral demands. To paraphrase the argument that motivates this example: equal parts Ayn Rand and John Calvin, your only duty is to yourself, and you are never doing well enough.

We can begin investigating that interpretation with a narrower measurement task: classify when an instructor gives technical coaching, talks about music, or encourages the rider, then consider what those categories say about the larger argument.

Burton motivates the research question. The three speech types here are illustrative teaching labels, not the class list recovered from the original dataset.

2. Begin with a sentence, not a philosophy

The supplied teaching script ends by testing the three sentences shown here: given a sentence, return a speech category. Its training CSV and saved model were not supplied, so these sentences do not come with verified model predictions.

For the explanation, we use the simplified categories Coaching, Music, and Encouragement; the real script constructs its label list from the available labeled data.

These are the script’s new test sentences. Their role in that script is prediction after training, not training examples.

3. A larger model supplies provisional labels

The workflow first asks Gemini Flash to assign topic labels to transcript sentences, creating paired inputs and targets for a smaller classifier to learn. In the diagram, an invented training sentence receives an illustrative Coaching label.

Learning to reproduce these labels establishes agreement with the labeling rule, so checking a sample for the distinctions a researcher cares about remains part of defining the measure.

The 30 observations and the three categories are schematic. The sentence “Keep your shoulders relaxed.” is an invented training example.

4. Reserve sentences before fitting the classifier

The script makes an 80/20 training–validation split stratified by topic: each category contributes examples to both parts, and the reserved sentences supply no gradient updates. Our schematic corpus has ten observations per category, so eight move to training and two remain for validation.

This asks whether the classifier can reproduce labels on held-out sentences from this corpus; to assess generalization to new instructors or classes, we would also need a split that holds those groups apart.

30 illustrative observations = 24 training + 6 validation

5. Turn a training sentence into model inputs

The tokenizer turns a sentence into token IDs, adds the special tokens expected by the pretrained model, and supplies an attention mask so padding is ignored. The script uses the uncased tokenizer and truncates long inputs to a maximum of 128 tokens.

Token IDs identify vocabulary entries rather than meaningful coordinates; the pretrained encoder supplies the learned representations that make them useful.

The displayed token sequence is schematic; token IDs are deliberately omitted. It is not a captured tokenizer output.

6. Start from an encoder that already represents language

DistilBERT begins with pretrained parameters rather than learning language from these fitness transcripts alone. Its contextual representations let the same word contribute differently depending on the surrounding sentence.

For sentence classification, the first token’s final hidden state passes into a task-specific classification head, which turns this representation into scores for the chosen speech categories.

sentence → tokens → pretrained encoder → sentence representation h

7. The classification head turns a representation into scores

The head produces one logit for each category: an unconstrained score that need not lie between zero and one or sum with the other scores to one. To make the update visible, we now use a tiny differentiable surrogate with a two-dimensional hidden representation and a three-category linear head.

Its weights, scores, and probabilities are teaching calculations, not outputs from the unavailable DistilBERT model.

Tiny surrogate: h = tanh(E x),   z = W h + b

8. Softmax makes a distribution; the label defines a loss

Softmax converts the three logits into positive probabilities that sum to one. For an example labeled Coaching, cross-entropy is the negative logarithm of the probability assigned to Coaching: a confident correct assignment has a smaller loss.

The supplied target label defines this penalty, so reducing the loss teaches agreement with that label rather than independently establishing the validity of the measure.

pk = exp(zk) / Σj exp(zj),   L = −log pCoaching
All displayed probabilities and loss values are calculated from the explicit tiny surrogate and are illustrative.

9. One loss sends a gradient through both parts

Backpropagation follows how the loss depends on the head and then on the encoder, supplying gradients for a parameter update. Scroll through the step to see the surrogate’s encoder and head weights move together, increasing its Coaching probability and reducing this example’s loss.

Both parts are trainable in the supplied DistilBERT workflow: pretrained weights provide a starting point and can change during fine-tuning.

θ ← θ − η ∇θL,   θ = {encoder parameters, head parameters}
This is a full gradient update in the tiny surrogate. It illustrates the dependency structure, not the numerical size of an actual DistilBERT update.

10. Fine-tuning can update more than the new head

A frozen encoder supplies fixed features while only the head learns; full fine-tuning lets both the encoder and the head adapt to the task. The supplied script uses full fine-tuning and does not freeze the encoder.

The comparison clarifies which parameters are being trained: adding a new classification head does not, by itself, imply that only the head changes.

The frozen setup is a counterfactual teaching comparison. Full encoder-and-head training matches the supplied script.

11. Repeat the updates over the training portion

An epoch is a pass through the training examples. The supplied run schedules four epochs using minibatches of sixteen examples, evaluating the reserved portion after each epoch as the pretrained representation adapts to the labeling task.

Four passes describe the procedure; they do not tell us the resulting validation accuracy or guarantee that another pass would help.

training minibatches → parameter updates → next epoch; validation supplies no gradients
The four passes come from the script. The moving observations are a schematic corpus, not its actual minibatches.

12. Now ask how the model labels reserved sentences

At validation, the fitted model predicts without updating its parameters; comparison with the reserved labels yields validation loss, overall accuracy, and accuracy within each topic. The script uses validation results to select a saved checkpoint, making this a model-selection set rather than a separate untouched test set for a final performance estimate.

No empirical score is shown here because the data and trained weights were not supplied.

The script’s three short demonstration sentences are useful probes, but they are not a substitute for a labeled test set.

13. Inspect the representation without mistaking a picture for a score

The script also extracts the first token’s final hidden state for each training sentence, before the classification head, and projects those vectors into two dimensions to inspect the distinctions their representation makes. The positions shown here are illustrative, not a computed projection.

Even a real picture of separated training clusters would not establish held-out accuracy: a two-dimensional map discards information, and these are observations the model has already used.

Only the schematic training observations enter this picture. The original script uses a t-SNE projection of its training embeddings.

14. Return from predicted categories to the research question

A usable speech classifier could help describe how instructors divide their time among types of utterance, provided we examine mislabeled and ambiguous sentences before treating those categories as measurements. Burton’s broader interpretation concerns the moral language of self-love and self-improvement.

Coaching, Music, and Encouragement do not measure that argument by themselves; the analysis must connect its categories to the substantive claim rather than let a convenient classifier define the question.

This demonstration explains the training workflow. It reports no estimated topic frequencies, trained-model predictions, or empirical validation results.
