01 / THE PREDICTION TASK

From a score difference to a winner.

Show the alien the final score difference—Knicks points minus Spurs points—and ask whether the Knicks won. It must learn the connection from five games for which we supply both the scores and the answer.

The input is the point differential, x. The target is y = 1 for a Knicks win and y = 0 for a loss. These five labeled games are its training sample.

The game is already over. The alien is learning to classify the result from the final score, rather than forecast it before play begins.

02 / THE LEARNING RULE

It puts a boundary in the gap.

The closest observed loss is −4. The closest observed win is +1. The alien puts its cutoff halfway between them: −1.5.

It now predicts a win whenever x is at least −1.5. All five training outcomes fall on the correct side of that boundary.

We will give every alien this same learning procedure. Only the games it observes will change.

03 / A NEW GAME

The fit is perfect. The rule is wrong.

Now show the alien a game in which the Knicks lost by one point. At x = −1, its learned rule predicts a win.

The true boundary is zero. For −1.5 ≤ x < 0, the learned rule makes the same error: the alien calls it a win even though the Knicks did not outscore the Spurs.

The correct boundary was available in the model class. The sample simply did not pin it down.

04 / THREE TRAINING HISTORIES

Different games, different boundaries.

Keep Alien 1’s original five games and its cutoff of −1.5. Give two other aliens different training histories. All three apply the same learning procedure.

Alien 2 learns a different cutoff. Alien 3 sees five wins and no losses, so it puts its cutoff at −∞ and predicts a win for every finite point differential.

Give Alien 2 new games to see how its cutoff changes. Aliens 1 and 3 keep their histories.

What if all five outcomes are the same?

For a history containing only wins, use cutoff −∞; for only losses, use +∞. These rules predict the observed outcome for every finite input. Otherwise, use the midpoint cutoff and classify equality with it as a win.

05 / ONE FIXED INPUT

Ask them the same question.

In our synthetic games, the Knicks’ final score is half a point higher than the Spurs’. Who won?

Hold this test input fixed at x0 = +0.5. The actual outcome is y0 = 1. Each alien predicts a win when this input is at or to the right of its cutoff, and a loss when it is below the cutoff.

The figure zooms around the test input, with the same vertical line through all three rules. Any disagreement comes from their different training histories, because they answer the same question with the same learning procedure.

06 / A POPULATION OF FITTED RULES

Repeat the training 1,000 times.

Now run a controlled simulation. Give 1,000 aliens independently drawn five-game samples and ask each about the same half-point final difference.

In this worked simulation, 468 predict a loss and 532 predict a win. Each mark represents one independently trained alien.

Next, gather the marks at their predictions, zero or one. The axis will show the aliens’ answers, rather than the game’s point differential.

How the games are simulated

Draw each team’s score independently and uniformly from the integers 0 through 100, then add half a point to the Knicks’ score to prevent ties. Fit the same cutoff rule to each five-game sample.

The numerical proportions depend on this score distribution. The three earlier histories illustrate possible fitted rules; this repeated experiment estimates their behavior under the stated generator.

07 / EXPECTATION

The average lives between their answers.

Gather the aliens by their predictions: 468 at zero and 532 at one. Their average prediction is 0.532.

An expectation over training samples averages predictions from datasets drawn in the same way, each fitted by the same procedure. Our 1,000 repeated fits estimate that expectation for the synthetic game generator.

No individual alien answers 0.532. It is the average across fitted rules at one fixed input.

Write this expectation in notation

𝒟 denotes one alien’s training dataset, and f^𝒟 is the rule fitted to it. The expectation varies the training dataset while keeping the test input x0 fixed.

𝔼𝒟[f^𝒟(x0)]≈0.532

08 / BIAS

Measure from the truth to the average.

The true outcome is one. The average prediction is 0.532, which is 0.468 below it.

Bias is the expected prediction minus the truth. Our estimate is therefore −0.468; its square is approximately 0.219.

At this winning input, every wrong answer lies below the truth. These errors cannot cancel when we average them. Squaring the bias gives its contribution to mean squared error.

09 / VARIANCE

Measure each answer from the average.

Keep the same predictions and the same mean. This time, measure how far each prediction lies from 0.532.

The 468 loss predictions are 0.532 below the mean. The 532 win predictions are 0.468 above it. Square those distances and average them to estimate variance: approximately 0.249.

For zero-or-one predictions, this spread is largest at an even split, where variance is 0.25. Our aliens are close to that split. Greater agreement among their answers would reduce this variance.

10 / MEAN SQUARED ERROR

Now measure each answer from the truth.

The 532 win predictions are correct, so their squared errors are zero. The 468 loss predictions are one unit from the truth, so their squared errors are one.

Average over all 1,000 aliens. Mean squared error is 468 / 1,000 = 0.468: the fraction of wrong answers.

Call the probability that a newly trained alien is wrong q. Our repeated fits estimate q as 0.468. This single probability will connect squared bias and variance.

The predictions have not changed. We have changed the reference point: from their mean, for variance, to the actual outcome, for MSE.

11 / THE DECOMPOSITION

One probability sets both parts.

Squared bias measures how far the average prediction is from the truth. Variance measures how much the individual predictions spread around that average.

At this winning input, the average prediction is 1 − q, so bias is −q and squared bias is q². Variance is q(1 − q). Their sum is q, the MSE: approximately 0.219 + 0.249 = 0.468.

Outcome noise is zero because the final score differential determines the winner.

For q below one half, reducing q brings the average closer to the truth and makes the predictions more unanimous. Squared bias and variance then fall together.

We have kept the learning rule and its complexity fixed. More informative training samples can reduce both sources of error; there is no necessary tradeoff between them here.

The exact worked values

Squared bias is 0.219024 and variance is 0.248976. Their sum is 0.468. These are Monte Carlo estimates, but the decomposition holds for this finite population of predictions, using divisor 1,000 for variance.

Give the aliens more experience.

When variance can initially rise

If more than half the aliens are wrong, reducing q toward 0.5 lowers squared bias but raises variance. The answers become less unanimously wrong before they become mostly correct. This follows from binary predictions; it does not compare models of different complexity.

At a losing test input, bias has the opposite sign, +q. Squared bias q², variance q(1 − q), and MSE q retain the same relationship.
