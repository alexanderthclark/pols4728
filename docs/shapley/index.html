A SCROLLING EXPLAINER / MAJORITY RULE

Three votes, one outcome

A proposal needs two yes votes. All three voters support it. Everyone helped—but how much credit should each receive?

02

Two votes are enough

Groups with two or three voters pass (1). Groups with one or no voters fail (0).

03

Start with small coalitions. The proposal fails.

04

The second vote decides

Every pair passes and has value one. Adding A to B changes failure into success: a contribution of one.

05

Order changes the credit

In the order B → A → C, A supplies the deciding vote. Change the order and a different voter gets the credit.

06

Give every order equal weight

Average across all six equally weighted orders. A’s four joining edges appear in 2, 1, 1, and 2 orders.

07

Average A’s contributions

Multiply each contribution by its fraction of orders, then add. A receives one-third of the total credit.

08

Explore the whole picture

B and C receive one-third too. Their credits sum to one. Select a voter, inspect a group, or replay an imagined order.

09

Name each coalition’s value

Write a coalition’s value as v⁡(S), where |S| counts its voters. This rule assigns one to passing groups and zero otherwise.

1 means passes. 0 means fails. The proposal always needs two votes.

10

Write the weighted average

The Shapley value ϕi⁡(v) averages player i’s contribution. Sum over every coalition S that excludes i: one term for each joining edge.

11

Read one marginal contribution

The bracket is the change when A joins S. For S={B}, the proposal changes from failing to passing: 1−0=1.

12

Count orders before A

Hold S fixed. Its members can arrive before A in |S|! orders. With S={B,C}, that means B → C or C → B.

13

Count orders after A

After S and A arrive, n−|S|−1 voters remain. Their factorial counts the possible orders after A.

14

Turn counts into weights

Multiply the before and after counts, then divide by all n! orders. The result is the fraction using this joining edge.

15

Add the weighted contributions

The formula adds the same four weighted contributions: A’s Shapley value is one-third. For another game, change the value function.

Find the pivotal state

Treat the 50 states and DC as 51 blocs. The state that first reaches 270 is pivotal. Its Shapley value is its fraction of equally weighted orders supplying the deciding vote.

The voting model

The players are the 50 states and DC, using the electoral votes allocated for 2024 and 2028: 538 in total, with 270 needed to win. State i has wi electoral votes.

Treat each state as one bloc, including Maine and Nebraska, which can split their votes in actual elections. These are accounting orders, not the chronology of an election.

Give all orders equal weight. A state’s fraction of pivotal orders is its Shapley–Shubik power index.

Adjusted electoral votes

Express pivotal power on the same 538-vote scale: adjusted votes equal 538 times a state’s Shapley value. Equivalently, multiply its electoral votes by its power-to-vote-share ratio. Both cartograms use the same area per vote.

The squares keep their size. Whole squares represent whole adjusted votes; a fractional boundary square represents the remainder. California’s 54 squares become about 58.1 square units, adding four whole squares and part of another. Texas’s 40 become about 41.7; Wyoming’s three become about 2.91. The total remains 538 adjusted votes.

The dashed outline retains the selected state’s electoral-vote area. “Outline electoral-vote areas” adds the other states’ outlines for comparison. The displayed area changes use their actual proportions.

California has 10.04% of the electoral votes but 10.80% of the pivotal credit. Texas’s premium is about 4.1%.

A larger bloc can cross 270 from a wider range of losing totals. For this allocation, California, Texas, Florida, and New York have power above their electoral vote shares. Each three-vote state and DC has about 3.1% less.

This measures structural power under equally weighted state orders. It does not account for partisan lean or competitiveness, or measure an individual voter’s influence.

How the exact values are calculated

For each state, count coalitions of the other 50 states by their number of members and their electoral vote total. A coalition makes the state pivotal when its total is below 270 but adding the state reaches 270.

Weight each count by the arrangements before and after the state, exactly as in the Shapley formula. Dynamic programming evaluates this sum with exact integer counts; it requires neither simulation nor a list of all 51! orders. Displayed decimals are rounded.

Allocations: National Archives. Exact Electoral College analysis: Mann and Shapley (1962).
